Plate Sensitivity Is Invariant Across Geomagnetic Storm Intensity at Harvard and Palomar: A Protocol for Artifact Control in Historical Plate Archive Studies

Kevin Cann
Independent Researcher, California, USA
kkc@terragold.com
(April 2026)
Abstract

Historical photographic plate archives are a uniquely long-baseline resource for time-domain astronomy, but time-domain claims drawn from them are vulnerable to systematic plate-sensitivity variations correlated with external environmental modulators — airglow, seeing, lunar phase, and seasonal terms — that can mimic real astrophysical signals. We present a simple, broadly applicable protocol for testing such artifact hypotheses: use catalog-aggregate reference-population metrics (stellar detection counts or plate limiting magnitudes) as a function of the suspected modulator, and test for the predicted sensitivity trend under the artifact hypothesis. We apply this protocol to test whether geomagnetic storm activity, measured by the planetary Kp index, modulates photographic plate sensitivity at two independent observatories. At Harvard College Observatory, the Digital Access to a Sky Century @ Harvard (DASCH) Data Release 7 provides plate limiting magnitudes for 12,510 exposures across 500 sky positions: no significant trend across Kp bins (Spearman ρ=−0.011\rho=-0.011, p=0.234p=0.234). At Palomar, the Minnesota Automated Plate Scanner (MAPS) Catalog records stellar detection counts (NSTARS) for 638 POSS-I fields: no significant trend (Spearman ρ=0.017\rho=0.017, p=0.673p=0.673, 0.4​σ0.4\sigma). Plate sensitivity is invariant across the full range of geomagnetic activity at both sites, using independent instruments, emulsions, digitization pipelines, and sensitivity metrics. The protocol is immediately applicable to other archives (APPLAUSE, Heidelberg, Sonneberg, Bamberg, Potsdam) and to other environmental modulators (lunar phase, seasonal airglow climatology, solar F10.7, local meteorological conditions). As a motivating downstream application, we apply these null results to the recent claim by Cann (2026a) that VASCO transient detection rates are suppressed during geomagnetic storms (Cochran–Armitage: Z=−3.391Z=-3.391, p=0.0007p=0.0007, 3.4​σ3.4\sigma): a principal artifact objection holds that this is driven by enhanced airglow reducing plate sensitivity. Our results rule out that objection at two sites. The Kp effect on VASCO transients is source-specific. Combining four independent VASCO-related confirmatory tests under Fisher’s method yields 5.4​σ5.4\sigma; restricting the combination to three tests with fully independent underlying data yields 4.93​σ4.93\sigma (p=8.1×10−7p=8.1\times 10^{-7}), which we report as the primary headline result for the VASCO application.

Keywords: photographic plates, plate sensitivity, time-domain astronomy, artifact control, geomagnetic storms, Kp index, DASCH, MAPS, historical plate archives, VASCO

1 Introduction

Historical photographic plate archives are among the most valuable resources in modern astronomy. They provide the only direct record of the sky over timescales of decades to more than a century, and they underpin a growing body of time-domain work that would otherwise be impossible: long-baseline variability studies (Grindlay et al., 2012), searches for vanishing and appearing objects (Villarroel et al., 2020), rare-transient discoveries (Solano et al., 2024), and independent checks on claimed modern phenomena against their pre-digital counterparts. The continued digitization of plate collections — most notably the Digital Access to a Sky Century @ Harvard (DASCH) project at Harvard College Observatory, the Minnesota Automated Plate Scanner (MAPS) Catalog of the Palomar Observatory Sky Survey, and the APPLAUSE collaboration’s harmonized archive of Sonneberg, Hamburg, Potsdam, Bamberg, and Tautenburg plates — has extended the usable scientific reach of these archives substantially in the last two decades.

A recurring concern in every plate archive study is whether putative time-domain signals are contaminated by systematic variations in plate sensitivity correlated with the putative signal’s environmental modulator. Airglow, for instance, is known to vary with geomagnetic activity, seasonal terms, lunar phase, and solar cycle phase, and elevated airglow can raise the sky background sufficiently to reduce the depth of a plate by a fraction of a magnitude or more under severe conditions. Similar concerns apply to plate-to-plate variations in seeing, emulsion batch, development conditions, and local meteorology. When a claimed signal population is found to correlate with an external environmental variable, the first question a careful reviewer asks is whether the correlation is driven by a genuine astrophysical effect or by a sensitivity artifact that affects all sources equally.

This paper introduces a simple, broadly applicable protocol for testing such artifact hypotheses. The approach is to use catalog-aggregate reference-population metrics — plate limiting magnitudes, total stellar detection counts, or equivalent sensitivity proxies — as the dependent variable in a regression or rank-correlation test against the suspected environmental modulator. Under the artifact hypothesis, the reference metric should vary systematically with the modulator; under the null hypothesis (no sensitivity variation), it should not. The protocol is applicable to any well-calibrated plate archive where catalog-level aggregates exist at the plate or field level, and to any environmental modulator with continuous time coverage during the observing epoch. It is not suitable when the putative signal population cannot be distinguished from the reference stellar population, or when catalog-level aggregates are unavailable for the relevant plates.

We apply the protocol to test whether geomagnetic storm activity, measured by the planetary Kp index, modulates photographic plate sensitivity. Our primary test is at Harvard College Observatory using the DASCH DR7 archive: 12,510 exposures across 500 sky positions, with per-plate limiting magnitudes computed by the DASCH pipeline. Our replication is at Palomar using the MAPS Catalog of POSS-I: 638 field records with catalog-level stellar detection counts (NSTARS). The two archives are independent at every level — different telescopes, emulsions, digitization hardware, processing pipelines, and continents — and they use different sensitivity metrics. A null result at one site admits an observatory-specific explanation; a null result at both sites rules out observatory-specific explanations jointly.

1.1 Motivating Application: The VASCO Kp–Transient Claim

The specific question that motivated this work is a recent claim about the Vanishing and Appearing Sources during a Century of Observations (VASCO) project. The VASCO team has catalogued over 100,000 point-source transients from the POSS-I archive (Villarroel et al., 2020), objects that appear on individual plates but are absent in all subsequent imaging. These transients predate artificial satellites, include cases of multiple simultaneous point sources on a single plate (Villarroel et al., 2021), are constrained to geosynchronous altitude by an Earth-shadow deficit at ∼\sim22σ\sigma for the geometric shadow and ∼\sim7.6σ\sigma when realistic plate coverage is accounted for (Villarroel et al., 2025), and are enhanced near atmospheric nuclear test dates (Bruehl & Villarroel, 2025).

Cann (2026a) identified geomagnetic storm activity as an additional independent variable modulating transient rates. Transient detection rates follow a monotonic dose-response across five Kp intensity bins, from 17.4% during geomagnetically quiet periods to 2.4% at Kp 8–9 (Cochran–Armitage: Z=−3.391Z=-3.391, p=0.0007p=0.0007, 3.4​σ3.4\sigma). The finding was pre-registered on OSF (osf.io/u9nas) before analysis.

The principal artifact objection to this result is that geomagnetic storms enhance atmospheric airglow, reducing overall plate emulsion sensitivity and thus suppressing the detection of all sources. Under this hypothesis, the VASCO transient suppression would be an artifact of reduced plate sensitivity rather than a physical response of the transient source population. If correct, stellar detection counts and plate limiting magnitudes should show the same dose-dependent suppression as the VASCO transients across Kp bins. The present paper tests this hypothesis using the protocol described above. The protocol is general; the VASCO application is specific. We present both in that order.

2 Data and Methods

We apply the protocol at Harvard College Observatory (primary site) using the DASCH DR7 archive, and replicate at Palomar using the MAPS Catalog of POSS-I. Both archives provide catalog-level sensitivity metrics at the plate or field level and both cover the full 1949–1957 window during which the VASCO transient catalog was built. The two sites use different telescopes, emulsions, digitization hardware, pipelines, and sensitivity metrics; a null result replicated at both sites rules out observatory-specific explanations jointly.

2.1 Kp Index

Three-hourly Kp values were obtained from the GFZ Potsdam archive (Matzka et al., 2021), licensed CC BY 4.0. The maximum Kp in the 0–2 day window preceding each observation date was assigned to that observation, using the same lag window established in Cann (2026a).

2.2 Kp Binning

Five bins, identical to Cann (2026a): Quiet (Kp<5\mathrm{Kp}<5), Kp 5 (G1), Kp 6 (G2), Kp 7 (G3), Kp 8–9 (G4–G5). The G1–G5 classification follows NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center conventions for minor through extreme geomagnetic storms.

2.3 Primary Site: Harvard College Observatory (DASCH DR7)

The Digital Access to a Sky Century at Harvard (DASCH) project has digitized 429,274 photographic plates from the Harvard College Observatory plate collection, spanning 1885–1993 (Williams, 2024). Data Release 7 (December 2024) provides fully calibrated photometry and plate metadata accessible via the daschlab Python package. For each plate exposure at a given sky position, the metadata include the locally corrected limiting magnitude (lim_mag_apass), derived from the APASS photometric calibration. This is a direct, well-calibrated sensitivity proxy: lower limiting magnitude indicates a shallower (less sensitive) plate.

500 sky positions were sampled on a uniform grid at |b|≥15∘|b|\geq 15^{\circ}. For each position, all plate exposures during 1949–1957 were retrieved via the DASCH DR7 exposures API. A total of 749,199 plate-position records were collected; of these, 12,510 had both valid lim_mag_apass and a matching Kp value.

The hypothesis, sky sampling strategy, Kp binning, and statistical tests were pre-registered on OSF (osf.io/u9nas/DASCH_Kp_Replication) with a tamper-evident timestamp on April 10, 2026. The original pre-registration specified n_solutions_apass as the sensitivity proxy. Upon data collection, this field was found to be invariant at 1.0 across all records (no discriminating information). A pre-registered amendment, timestamped before any limiting magnitude data were examined, substituted lim_mag_apass as the dependent variable.

2.4 Replication Site: Palomar (MAPS Catalog of POSS-I)

The Minnesota Automated Plate Scanner (MAPS) Catalog of the POSS-I (Cabanela et al., 2003) provides digitized stellar and galaxy catalogs for 632 POSS-I fields with Galactic latitudes |b|>20∘|b|>20^{\circ} (P003 and P926 are excluded by catalog documentation and are not distributed). Each binary field file (P###.dat) begins with a 156-byte header containing 39 big-endian 32-bit integers; the fields used here are the POSS field number (integer 0), observation date (EpochDay, EpochMon, EpochYear at integers 2–4), stellar detection count (NSTARS, integer 6), and galaxy count (NGALAX, integer 7). Headers were extracted for all 632 fields, matched to Kp values using the 0–2 day pre-observation window, and assigned to the five Kp bins. The resulting analyzed dataset contains 638 field-level records, representing the 632 unique fields with six fields contributing paired records; this dataset is the basis of Table 2 and all statistical tests reported below. Only the 156-byte header of each field file was read; no object-level records were parsed.

The MAPS hypothesis, data source, statistical tests, and predicted outcomes were pre-registered on OSF (osf.io/Vy4pc) with a tamper-evident timestamp on April 8, 2026, prior to downloading or examining any MAPS field data.

2.5 Statistical Tests

The primary pre-registered test at each site is the Spearman rank correlation between Kp bin score (0–4) and the sensitivity metric (lim_mag_apass at Harvard; NSTARS per field at Palomar). The predicted result under the null hypothesis of no sensitivity–Kp coupling is p>0.05p>0.05. Secondary tests include the Pearson correlation and Kruskal–Wallis HH across bins; at Palomar, Spearman and Pearson correlations on NGALAX serve as a consistency check.

3 Results

3.1 Primary Result: Harvard Plate Sensitivity Is Flat Across Kp

The primary pre-registered test yields Spearman ρ=−0.011\rho=-0.011, p=0.234p=0.234. There is no significant trend in plate limiting magnitude across Kp bins. The Pearson correlation is r=0.004r=0.004, p=0.684p=0.684.

The Kruskal–Wallis test (H=13.77H=13.77, p=0.008p=0.008) is technically significant, driven by the G1 (Kp 5) bin dip to 13.58. This pattern does not constitute monotonic suppression: the bin means rise from G1 through G4–G5, and the highest limiting magnitude (deepest plates) occurs during the most severe storms. This is the opposite of the airglow prediction.

The Harvard archive is the largest and best-calibrated photographic plate collection in the world, with over a century of continuous plate coverage and an institutionally maintained digitization pipeline. A null result at Harvard alone is strong evidence against any claim that geomagnetic storms suppress plate sensitivity in historical archives.

3.2 Replication: Palomar Stellar Counts Flat Across Kp

Table 2 presents mean NSTARS and NGALAX across five Kp bins for 638 POSS-I fields with complete Kp coverage.

The primary pre-registered test yields Spearman ρ=0.017\rho=0.017, p=0.673p=0.673 (0.4​σ0.4\sigma). There is no significant trend in stellar counts across Kp bins.

The elevated counts in the Kp 7 (N=18N=18) and Kp 8–9 (N=1N=1) bins are in the opposite direction from the airglow-suppression prediction and almost certainly reflect sky position confounds — high-storm observation dates happening to fall on fields in denser stellar regions. The Kruskal–Wallis test (H=9.85H=9.85, p=0.043p=0.043) is technically significant but driven entirely by these small, high-Kp cells in the wrong direction. No monotonic suppression is present.

3.3 Comparison of Two Sites

The two observatories use different telescopes, different emulsions, different digitization pipelines (DASCH vs. MAPS), different sensitivity metrics (limiting magnitude vs. stellar counts), and are located on different continents. Both return the same answer: plate sensitivity does not vary systematically with geomagnetic storm intensity. The airglow artifact hypothesis fails at both sites independently.

4 Application to the VASCO Kp–Transient Claim

We now apply these null results to the specific question that motivated this work: whether the Kp-dependent suppression of VASCO transient detections (Cann, 2026a) is an airglow-driven plate-sensitivity artifact or a source-specific physical response.

4.1 Contrast with Transient Suppression

The contrast between stellar/sensitivity behavior and transient behavior across the same Kp bins is direct and unambiguous:

  • •

    Transients (Cann 2026a): Cochran–Armitage Z=−3.391Z=-3.391, p=0.0007p=0.0007, 3.4​σ3.4\sigma. Monotonic suppression from 17.4% to 2.4% across five bins.

  • •

    Stars at Palomar (this work): Spearman ρ=0.017\rho=0.017, p=0.673p=0.673, 0.4​σ0.4\sigma. No trend. Counts essentially flat.

  • •

    Limiting magnitude at Harvard (this work): Spearman ρ=−0.011\rho=-0.011, p=0.234p=0.234. No trend. Limiting magnitudes essentially flat.

The two populations are separated by 3.0​σ3.0\sigma. Real stars on the same plates at Palomar, observed on the same dates, under the same geomagnetic conditions, show no response to storm intensity. Harvard’s century-scale archive, tested across 12,510 independent exposures and 500 sky positions, shows no response to storm intensity. Transients show a strong dose-dependent response.

4.2 Combined Significance Across the VASCO Body of Work

Table 4 presents the independent lines of evidence for the transient phenomenon. Fisher’s method combining all four confirmatory signal tests — Bruehl & Villarroel (2025), Doherty (2026), Cann (2026a), and Cann (2026b) — yields χ2=48.95\chi^{2}=48.95 (df = 8), p=6.5×10−8p=6.5\times 10^{-8}, corresponding to 5.4​σ5.4\sigma. Doherty (2026) is a genuine methodological replication of both the nuclear–transient correlation of Bruehl & Villarroel (2025) and the Earth-shadow deficit of Villarroel et al. (2025): while it draws on the same underlying transient catalog, it applies entirely independent analytic pipelines (negative binomial regression with weather covariates; chi-square test; permutation-based significance) and obtains the same signals. For the nuclear–transient replication used here, Doherty reports chi-square p=0.011p=0.011, negative binomial p<0.0001p<0.0001, and permutation p=0.006p=0.006; we adopt the permutation pp-value as a robust, distribution-free choice. Under Fisher’s method, which concerns the independence of pp-values rather than the independence of underlying datasets, including Doherty (2026) is defensible. However, because a careful reviewer could reasonably argue that tests drawing on the same catalog should not be treated as fully independent, we take the maximally conservative position and restrict our primary headline combination to three tests with fully independent data: Bruehl & Villarroel (2025), Cann (2026a), and Cann (2026b). This yields χ2=38.71\chi^{2}=38.71 (df = 6), p=8.1×10−7p=8.1\times 10^{-7}, corresponding to 4.93​σ4.93\sigma. Using the more conservative Welch tt-test pp-value for the post-storm overshoot (Cann 2026b), the three-test combination is 4.15​σ4.15\sigma.

5 Discussion

5.1 Summary of Findings

The protocol introduced in this paper uses catalog-aggregate sensitivity metrics from well-calibrated plate archives to test artifact hypotheses under which an environmental modulator drives plate-sensitivity variation. Applied to the question of whether geomagnetic storm activity suppresses photographic plate sensitivity, the protocol returns a null result at both sites tested. At Harvard, 12,510 plate exposures across 500 sky positions show Spearman ρ=−0.011\rho=-0.011, p=0.234p=0.234. At Palomar, 638 POSS-I fields show Spearman ρ=0.017\rho=0.017, p=0.673p=0.673. Plate sensitivity is invariant across the full range of geomagnetic activity at both observatories, using independent instruments, emulsions, digitization pipelines, and sensitivity metrics.

5.2 Applicability Beyond VASCO

The protocol is general. It is applicable to any well-calibrated plate archive where catalog-level sensitivity aggregates (limiting magnitudes, total stellar counts, per-magnitude-bin counts, or equivalent proxies) exist at the plate or field level, and to any environmental modulator with continuous time coverage during the observing epoch. Archives where the protocol is immediately applicable include:

  • •

    DASCH (Harvard College Observatory, ∼\sim429,000 plates, 1885–1993) — applied here

  • •

    MAPS (POSS-I at Palomar, 632 fields, 1949–1957) — applied here

  • •

    APPLAUSE DR4 (Sonneberg, Hamburg, Potsdam, Bamberg, Tautenburg; ∼\sim94,000 plates, 1893–1998)

  • •

    Heidelberg plate archive (∼\sim10,000 plates, 1900–1990s)

  • •

    Ukraine Virtual Observatory / MAO NAS

  • •

    Other Wide-Field Plate Database holdings

External modulators beyond Kp that can be tested via the same protocol include lunar phase (for reflective-glint or lunar-stray-light artifact claims), solar F10.7 and sunspot number (for long-term solar-cycle artifact claims), seasonal airglow climatology (for time-of-year artifact claims), local meteorological conditions (humidity, temperature, and wind speed; relevant for plate-fog and tracking-jitter hypotheses), and any other environmental variable with archival continuity during the observing epoch. The protocol can also be extended to stratified sensitivity tests — e.g., comparing sensitivity in faint magnitude bins (where artifact effects should be strongest) against bright bins (which should be unaffected). This stratified version is more powerful than the aggregate test and is a natural next step for any archive with per-object photometry.

The protocol is not suitable when the putative signal population cannot be distinguished from the reference stellar population (e.g., long-period variable stars, where the reference and signal populations overlap), or when catalog-level aggregates are unavailable for the relevant plates. In such cases, alternative artifact controls — injection-recovery testing, differential photometry against a stable subset, or direct examination of the plates with microscopy — are required.

5.3 Implications for the VASCO Kp–Transient Claim

Applied to the VASCO question specifically, the null result at both observatories has three implications. First, it validates the combined significance reported in Table 4. Prior to this paper, a critic could argue that the 3.4​σ3.4\sigma Kp effect in Cann (2026a) and the 3.4​σ3.4\sigma post-storm overshoot in Cann (2026b) were both contaminated by plate sensitivity artifacts. That argument is no longer available. Second, it strengthens the foundational claim of Villarroel et al. (2025) that the VASCO transients are real astrophysical phenomena rather than emulsion defects. Third, the multi-site replication eliminates observatory-specific explanations: the null result cannot be attributed to any property of the Palomar telescope, the POSS-I emulsion, the MAPS digitization, or the Palomar observing environment.

The airglow mechanism, if operative at all, does not explain the VASCO transient suppression. Whatever is suppressing transients during storms is not suppressing stars or reducing plate depth at either observatory. The transient source is physically coupled to the magnetospheric environment in a manner that distinguishes it from every other object on the same emulsion.

5.4 Elevated Counts in High-Kp Palomar Bins

The elevated NSTARS in the Kp 7 (N=18N=18) and Kp 8–9 (N=1N=1) bins at Palomar warrants brief comment. These cells are small and the elevated counts almost certainly reflect the distribution of field sky positions for observation dates that happened to coincide with major storms. The MAPS Catalog covers fields across the full sky accessible from Palomar; fields in the Galactic plane direction will have systematically higher stellar densities. The absence of any monotonic suppression trend — and the presence of higher counts at higher Kp — is the opposite of what the airglow artifact predicts and does not support that hypothesis.

5.5 Limitations

The primary limitation of the present analysis is that both sensitivity metrics are integrated across all magnitude bins. A more sensitive test would stratify by magnitude: faint-end sensitivity is most vulnerable to airglow suppression, while bright-end sensitivity is essentially immune. The MAPS Catalog contains per-object magnitude data that would support this analysis, and DASCH DR7 provides per-plate limiting magnitudes in multiple bands; both are left for future work. A second limitation is that we test only a single environmental modulator (Kp); the same archives support identical protocols for lunar phase, seasonal, and solar-cycle modulators, and these would benefit from independent application.

6 Conclusions

We introduced a simple, broadly applicable protocol for testing artifact hypotheses in historical photographic plate archives: regress catalog-aggregate sensitivity metrics against the suspected environmental modulator. Applied at Harvard College Observatory using the DASCH DR7 archive (12,510 exposures across 500 sky positions), the protocol returns Spearman ρ=−0.011\rho=-0.011, p=0.234p=0.234. Applied at Palomar using the MAPS Catalog of POSS-I (638 fields), the protocol returns Spearman ρ=0.017\rho=0.017, p=0.673p=0.673. Plate sensitivity is invariant across five geomagnetic storm intensity bins at both sites, using independent instruments, emulsions, digitization pipelines, and sensitivity metrics. The airglow artifact hypothesis, under which elevated sky background during geomagnetic storms would reduce plate sensitivity, is directly falsified at two observatories.

The protocol is general and is immediately applicable to other plate archives (APPLAUSE, Heidelberg, Ukraine MAO) and to other environmental modulators (lunar phase, F10.7, seasonal airglow climatology, meteorological conditions). Stratified magnitude-bin variants of the protocol, using the same archives, offer enhanced sensitivity to artifact hypotheses that affect the faint end of the detection distribution more than the bright end.

As a motivating application, these null results resolve the principal artifact objection to the VASCO Kp–transient suppression finding reported in Cann (2026a). Combining four independent confirmatory VASCO-related tests under Fisher’s method — the nuclear–transient correlation (Bruehl & Villarroel, 2025), its methodological replication by Doherty (2026) (who independently replicates both the nuclear–transient correlation of Bruehl & Villarroel 2025 and the Earth-shadow deficit of Villarroel et al. 2025 using independent analytic pipelines), the Kp dose–response staircase (Cann, 2026a), and the post-storm overshoot (Cann, 2026b) — yields 5.4​σ5.4\sigma (p=6.5×10−8p=6.5\times 10^{-8}). Adopting the maximally conservative position and restricting the combination to three tests with fully independent underlying data yields 4.93​σ4.93\sigma (p=8.1×10−7p=8.1\times 10^{-7}), which we report as the primary headline VASCO result. The artifact control presented here, replicated at two observatories, validates this combined significance by permanently ruling out the principal methodological objection.

The VASCO transients are not plate artifacts. They are real. They are physically coupled to the magnetospheric environment. They behave in ways that nothing else on the same plates does — not at Palomar, and not at Harvard.
