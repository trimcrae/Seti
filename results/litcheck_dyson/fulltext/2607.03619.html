Abstract

We report on the diagnostic inspection of nine Project Hephaistos Dyson Sphere candidate M-dwarfs based on archival data. By comparing the Gaia positions, propagated to the AllWISE epoch, with the mid-infrared centroids measured from the AllWISE images, together with deep archival optical/near-infrared imaging, we identified significant background contamination in candidates B and C—candidate B coincident with a radio counterpart with spectral index α=0.63±0.11\alpha=0.63\pm 0.11, and candidate C with a near-infrared companion at a 3.75​″3.75\arcsec offset. Candidate A provides suggestive evidence through a radio counterpart with α=0.40±0.35\alpha=0.40\pm 0.35, while candidates E, F, H and J show some marginal evidence. These systems exhibit either significant astrometric offsets or visible interlopers, indicating that the mid-infrared excess likely arises from line-of-sight contamination by hot, dust-obscured galaxies. However, candidates D and I still lack obvious signs of contamination. Dedicated observations are therefore essential to characterise these potential interlopers, in order to eliminate false positives and ensure that techno-signature searches focus on the most robust Dyson Sphere candidates.

1 Introduction

In mid-2024, Project Hephaistos111https://www.astro.uu.se/~ez/hephaistos/hephaistos.html reported seven Dyson sphere candidates. Dyson spheres are hypothetical megastructures envisioned to harvest stellar energy, with their most distinctive observable signature being excess infrared emission produced by the re-radiation of absorbed starlight as thermal waste heat (Dyson, 1960; Wright, 2020). The project identified these candidates by identifying stars with significant mid-infrared (MIR) excess from a sample of approximately 5 million stars detected by Gaia, 2MASS, and WISE, all within 300 parsecs of us (Suazo et al., 2024). All candidates are M-dwarf stars exhibiting MIR excess in the WISE W3 and W4 bands, with spectral energy distributions (SEDs) consistent with blackbody temperatures in the range of 100–300 K, as expected from partial Dyson Sphere models (Suazo et al., 2022).

Just as radio SETI surveys targeting nearby stars frequently encompass background extragalactic objects within the telescope’s field of view (Garrett and Siemion, 2023), this same principle applies analogously to infrared SETI searches. The cross-matching of seven candidates with archival radio surveys revealed offset radio counterparts for candidates A, B, and G (Ren, Garrett, and Siemion, 2024). High-resolution e-MERLIN (1.5 GHz) and EVN (5 GHz) observations (Ren, Garrett, and Siemion, 2025) confirmed that J2335−-0004, the radio source associated with candidate G, is a high brightness temperature (>1×108​K\rm>1\times 10^{8}~K) background active galactic nucleus (AGN) located more than 5 arcseconds from the Gaia position of the star in 2025. We did not detect radio emission exceeding 3​σ3\sigma at the stellar coordinates at 1.5 or 5 GHz, and thus found no evidence of broadband radio emission at these frequencies that could represent unintentional radio leakage from the candidate system.

Furthermore, the positional offset analysis of the ALLWISE images revealed that while the emission in the WISE W1 and W2 bands is associated with the M-dwarf, the W3 and W4 emission is primarily contributed by the offset AGN. The spectral energy distribution (SED) of the AGN suggested that it was most likely a Hot Dust-Obscured Galaxy (Hot DOG; Assef et al. 2015; Tsai et al. 2015) - bright in W3 and W4 bands but faint and obscured in the W1 and W2 bands.

Hot Dust-Obscured Galaxies (Hot DOGs) are a rare class of luminous galaxies powered by heavily obscured supermassive black holes, representing a distinct phase in galaxy evolution. Due to severe dust obscuration, they appear faint in the optical and near-infrared (NIR) but exhibit strong emission in the mid-infrared (MIR) as heated dust re-radiates energy. Consequently, Hot DOGs are faint in the WISE W1 and W2 bands but prominent in W3 and W4. When coincident with nearby M-dwarfs along the line of sight, the coarse 6–12 arcsec resolution of WISE results in blended photometry that can mimic the SED of a cold Dyson Sphere. Notably, Blain (2024) estimated the Hot DOG occurrence rate in the WISE catalogue, concluding that their surface density is sufficient to explain all seven reported Dyson Sphere candidates as chance alignments.

For the remaining six Dyson Sphere candidates of Suazo et al. (2024), two have radio counterparts (Ren, Garrett, and Siemion, 2024) that are also likely to be AGNs (candidates A and B). The nature of the other four (candidates C, D, E, and F) is unclear. Subsequently, further identification of the optical spectra of the Project Hephaistos stars led to the ancillary discovery of three additional Dyson Sphere candidates (H, I and J), which show significant MIR excesses in the W3 and W4 bands (Suazo (2024) and Korn and others (in prep.)).

Beyond searching for typical AGN signatures in potential background contaminants (e.g. high brightness temperature radio and X-ray emission), a complementary method involves identifying significant astrometric offsets across wavebands. By comparing WISE centroid positions with high-precision Gaia astrometry (accurate to microarcsecond scales), we can detect spatial offsets that serve as clear indicators of background contamination. This effect was explicitly observed in the analysis of Candidate G. Crucially, however, this approach relies on the background contaminant being spatially distinct from the foreground star; if the alignment is sufficiently precise, such offsets will remain unmeasurable. While Suazo et al. (2024) used W1 and W3 offsets to evaluate discrepancies and employed the Improved Reprocessing of the IRAS Survey (IRIS; Miville-Deschênes and Lagache 2005) maps at 100 μ\mum to mitigate potential contamination, residual contamination still exists in some cases, as proved by the radio observation results of candidate G (Ren, Garrett, and Siemion, 2025). Owing to the limited spatial resolution of WISE and IRAS, directly measuring the offset between MIR centroids across the four WISE bands and Gaia-measured stellar positions provides a more effective and precise diagnostic for identifying extragalactic contamination in apparent infrared-excess sources, yielding the best constraint attainable from existing data before high-resolution imaging observations.

Supplementing the primary data from Gaia (Gaia Collaboration et al., 2016, 2023), 2MASS (Skrutskie et al., 2006), and WISE (Wright et al., 2010), deeper archival imaging surveys cover several of the nine targets. These include optical surveys such as the Dark Energy Survey (DES; Dark Energy Survey Collaboration et al., 2016; DES DR1: Abbott et al., 2018; DES DR2: Abbott et al., 2021), the Panoramic Survey Telescope and Rapid Response System (Pan-STARRS; Chambers et al., 2016), SkyMapper (Onken et al., 2024), and the DESI Legacy Imaging Surveys (Dey et al., 2019), as well as NIR surveys such as the VISTA Science Archive (Cross et al., 2012), and the WFCAM Science Archive (Hambly et al., 2008). The greater depth of these images provides a valuable independent diagnostic, offering clues to potential faint background extragalactic objects contaminating the MIR flux of the Dyson Sphere candidates. The culprits responsible for the excess, though absent in shallower surveys like SDSS and 2MASS, may be detectable in these high-sensitivity archival images.

This paper presents a comprehensive assessment of the Dyson Sphere candidates through two complementary approaches: an astrometric diagnosis based on positional offsets and a detailed inspection of deep photometric surveys. The paper is structured as follows: in Section 2, we briefly describe the data and methods used in this paper. We present the results of our astrometric diagnostics in Section 3, followed by the archival image examination results in Section 4. Finally, we discuss our findings in Section 5 and summarise our conclusions in Section 6.

2 Data and Methods

2.1 Gaia Astrometry, Radio Counterparts, and WISE Centroid Measurement

We collected public archival data for nine M-dwarf candidates: six from Suazo et al. (2024) (S24 hereafter) and three from Suazo (2024) and Korn and others (in prep.) (K26 hereafter), all of which have Gaia DR3 source identifiers (see Table 1). We retrieved their high-precision astrometry, including coordinates and proper motions, for all nine stars from the Gaia DR3 database. Using these positions, we searched for counterparts at complementary wavelengths. Radio counterparts for candidates A and B were found in the predecessor identification (Ren, Garrett, and Siemion, 2024) via the VizieR access tool (Ochsenbein et al., 2000), while candidates C–F and X-ray counterparts for all candidates remained undetected. In this work, we updated the VizieR query to account for new radio survey data and cross-matched all positions with the High Energy Astrophysics Science Archive Research Center (HEASARC) database222https://heasarc.gsfc.nasa.gov/, which similarly yielded no additional detections.

To prepare the data for MIR centroid analysis in Section 3, we retrieved 1​′×1​′1\arcmin\times 1\arcmin image cutouts for each star in all four WISE bands from the ALLWISE atlas (WISE Team, 2020) via the NASA/IPAC Infrared Science Archive (IRSA)333https://irsa.ipac.caltech.edu/irsaviewer/. To refine the centroid analysis, we manually measured the signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs) for the four ALLWISE band images of each source, rather than adopting the pipeline-generated SNR values in the ALLWISE catalogue (Cutri et al., 2021). We used the sep.extract method from SEP Python library (Barbary, 2016), a re-implementation of SExtractor (Bertin and Arnouts, 1996), to detect the centroids of the ALLWISE maps. The W1 and W2 images, which have significantly higher SNRs, can generally be fitted successfully with SEP. We evaluated several alternative methods for detecting WISE spatial centroids and determined that SEP generally yields the best performance. However, for lower-resolution W3 and W4 images where if SEP fails to provide a successful fit, we adopted the scipy.ndimage.center_of_mass (Virtanen et al., 2020) function.

Neither sep.extract nor scipy.ndimage.center_of_mass provides direct centroid position uncertainties. We estimated these using the standard photon-limited centroiding precision formula:

σposition≈FWHMk×SNR\sigma_{\rm position}\approx\frac{\text{FWHM}}{k\times\text{SNR}} (1)

where k=2​2​ln⁡2≈2.355{k=2\sqrt{2\ln 2}\approx 2.355} (Howell, 2006) converts between Full Width at Half Maximum (FWHM) and Gaussian σ{\sigma}. We adopted WISE point spread function (PSF) FWHM of 6.1, 6.4, 6.5, and 12.0 arcseconds for W1–W4, respectively (Wright et al., 2010). The SNR at each centroid was manually measured via SEP aperture photometry. Therefore, all the centroiding uncertainties (σposition{\sigma_{\rm{position}}}) can be derived through this formula.

2.2 Epoch Propagation and Proper Motion Corrections

The extracted WISE centroids and their associated positional uncertainties reflect the state of the MIR emission at the specific epoch of the observations. As summarised in Table 1, the ALLWISE epochs for our targets span J2010.0 to J2011.0. Even within the same survey, the W3 and W4 band data were often collected slightly earlier than the W1 and W2 data. Because our multi-wavelength dataset compiles images from multiple independent surveys, these epoch differences can be substantial. Consequently, we rigorously accounted for the proper motions of the target stars when examining and cross-matching the spatial centroids in Section 3.

For instance, candidate A has a significant proper motion of 88.944​mas​yr−188.944~{\rm mas\,yr^{-1}}. While its RACS-high radio image was obtained at J2022.518, its mid-infrared images date back to the AllWISE deployment near J2010.5. This ∼\sim12 yr baseline introduces a purely kinematic positional displacement of 1.07​″1.07\arcsec. Propagating the stellar coordinates from the Gaia DR3 reference epoch (J2016.0) to the precise epoch of each individual map is therefore essential to prevent proper motion from distorting the true physical offsets. By executing this temporal alignment for every dataset, the final derived offset can reliably incorporate the centroiding uncertainty (σposition\sigma_{\rm position}) fully propagated alongside the intrinsic positional and proper motion errors of the Gaia stellar hosts and their corresponding radio counterparts.

2.3 Deep Optical and Near-Infrared Imaging Surveys

To examine the candidates using deep photometric and imaging data in Section 4, we queried several wide-field optical and NIR surveys, including the DES DR2, Pan-STARRS DR2, DESI Legacy Imaging Surveys (DR10), the UKIRT Infrared Deep Sky Survey (UKIDSS; Lawrence et al., 2007) and the UKIRT Hemisphere Survey (UHS; Dye et al., 2018) from the WFCAM Science Archive 444http://wsa.roe.ac.uk/, and two VISTA programmes from the VISTA Science Archive 555http://vsa.roe.ac.uk/: the Hemisphere Survey (VHS; DR5, McMahon et al. (2013))) and the Magellanic Clouds Survey (VMC; DR6, Cioni et al. (2011)). Table 2 summarises the central/effective wavelengths (or frequencies) and nominal angular resolutions of the optical and infrared datasets used in this work, together with the RACS radio surveys (Hale et al., 2021; Duchesne et al., 2024, 2025) covering candidates A and B. We find that all candidates are covered by the Legacy DR10 Survey, which provides the ideal optical imaging and serves as the primary dataset for this study. Additionally, six candidate stars have available VISTA images: A, B, E, F, H from the VHS, and C from the VMC. All candidates without VISTA coverage are accounted for by the Infrared Deep Sky Survey (UKIRT): D and I are covered by UKIDSS, and J is covered by the UHS. These Legacy optical images, combined with the NIR data from VISTA or UKIRT surveys, provide deep, multi-band imaging across all candidate fields. This enables a visual diagnosis entirely independent of our mid-infrared centroid analysis.

To provide identifiers for newly discovered or faint objects in each candidate’s images, we queried nearby NIR sources from the VISTA and UKIRT catalogues and optical sources from the Legacy DR10 Tractor catalogue. We also examined other major optical and NIR surveys, including DES, Pan-STARRS, SDSS, 2MASS, and SkyMapper. The DES images are already included in the DESI Legacy DR10 Imaging Surveys and thus are effectively represented in our Legacy analysis. The other surveys: Pan-STARRS, SDSS, 2MASS, and SkyMapper, have imaging quality and depth that are generally inferior to those of the Legacy, VISTA, and UKIRT datasets in the regions of interest. Our follow-up examination in Section 4 based on them did not yield additional detections of background or blended sources.

3 Astrometric Diagnostics

In this section, we analyse the positional offset between the MIR emission and the propagated Gaia position for each candidate star. As demonstrated by Ren, Garrett, and Siemion (2025), which analysed the case of candidate G, a significant offset between W3 & W4 emission spatial distribution to the star’s coordinate is the indicator of background contamination. For clarity, we divide our nine candidates into three subsamples based on their properties and origin:

  • •

    Candidates A and B, which are found to have radio counterparts. We additionally compare the radio counterpart positions with the WISE centroids and the propagated Gaia positions of the candidate stars.

  • •

    Candidates C, D, E, and F, the other four candidates drawn from S24 without radio counterparts.

  • •

    Candidates H, I, and J, the three newly identified targets from K26, also without radio counterparts.

The diagnostic process and results for each group are detailed in Subsections 3.1, 3.2, and 3.3. In Section 3.4, we compare the candidate centroiding results in this work with the offsets reported in S24. We then evaluate our astrometric approach in Subsection 3.5 by analysing control samples with W3 SNRs similar to those of the candidate stars.

3.1 Radio, WISE and Gaia astrometric diagnostics for candidates A and B

Candidates A (Gaia DR3 3496509309189181184) and B (Gaia DR3 4843191593270342656) are two of the three radio-associated sources (A, B, and G) reported by Ren, Garrett, and Siemion (2024); the radio source associated with G has been confirmed as an AGN (Ren, Garrett, and Siemion, 2025). Candidates A and B are located in the southern sky, with associated radio sources detected in RACS-DR1 (Hale et al., 2021) at 887.5 MHz with an angular resolution of 25″{\arcsec}. More recent RACS-mid (Duchesne et al., 2024) and RACS-high (Duchesne et al., 2025) observations at 1367.5 MHz and at 1655.5 MHz, provided significantly improved angular resolution compared to the original 25″ beam observations, enabling more precise comparison between radio source positions and stellar coordinates from Gaia. Figure 1 shows the positions of the radio sources A and B in different radio frequencies, and Table 3 shows the properties of the radio sources associated with candidate A and B.

Another supplementary dataset is the second data release for the GaLactic and Extragalactic All-sky Murchison Widefield Array eXtended (GLEAM-X)(Ross et al., 2024) which covers a frequency range of 72–231 MHz with twenty frequency bands. Although the angular resolution of GLEAM-X is at the arcminute level, it still effectively captures the fluxes of candidate B at lower frequencies. Combining GLEAM-X and RACS data extends the coverage from 87.5 to 1655.5 MHz. J0356-4031, the radio source associated with candidate B, has a high flux density exceeding 10 mJy at frequencies below 300 MHz and shows a steep spectral index (Sν∝ν−αS_{\nu}\propto\nu^{-\alpha}) of α87.51655.5=0.63±0.11\alpha^{1655.5}_{87.5}=0.63\pm 0.11 (Figure 2). The radio source J1245-2652 associated with candidate A, detected only in RACS, has a less well-constrained spectral index of α887.51655.5=0.40±0.35\alpha^{1655.5}_{887.5}=0.40\pm 0.35.

Using the ALLWISE centroid detection method described in Section 2, we obtained the MIR centroids for candidates A and B. All the W1, W2, and W3 band centroids were measured by the SEP, and both W4 images were measured by the scipy. These were then compared with their radio counterparts at 1367.5 MHz and 1655.5 MHz, a band that offers superior angular resolution and a circular beam. These WISE centroids were also compared with the stellar positions propagated using Gaia DR3 proper motions, accounting for the time offset between the ALLWISE observations and the radio measurement epochs. Figure 3 shows the locations of the stars, radio sources, and MIR centroids, and Table 4 lists the measured offsets between the Gaia, radio, and WISE centroids for candidates A and B.

After accounting for proper motion, candidate A shows a ∼3.3​″{\sim 3.3\arcsec} offset between the propagated Gaia position of the star and the 887.5 MHz radio source. This is smaller than 4.9″\arcsec reported in Ren, Garrett, and Siemion (2024), which did not correct for the star’s proper motion. At higher frequencies with improved angular resolution (1367.5 MHz and 1655.5 MHz), the radio source to stellar offset reduces to ∼\sim1.2″{\arcsec} and ∼\sim1.6″\arcsec (Table 3). The W1 and W2 centroids lie ∼1.6​″\sim 1.6\arcsec and ∼1.5​″\sim 1.5\arcsec from the propagated Gaia position of the candidate star A, respectively—closer than the corresponding offsets to the 1655.5 MHz radio source by over 2​″2\arcsec, but still much larger than the offsets to the 1367.5 MHz source by ∼1.2​″\sim 1.2\arcsec. In the W3 and W4 bands, the centroid–star offsets increase to about 2​″2\arcsec, which exceed all centroid–radio offsets at 1367.5 MHz and 1655.5 MHz (both ∼1.6​″\sim 1.6\arcsec). Combining the individual per-band measurements with inverse-variance weighting, the weighted-mean offset between the radio source and the epoch-corrected stellar (Gaia) position is 1.27±0.34​″1.27\pm 0.34\arcsec for candidate A and 2.64±0.36​″2.64\pm 0.36\arcsec for candidate B.

For candidate B, while the stellar-radio offset at 887.5 MHz is only ∼0.5​″\sim 0.5\arcsec, observations with smaller beam sizes at 1367.5 MHz and 1655.5 MHz frequencies reveal significantly larger radio to stellar offsets exceeding 2″\arcsec (Table 3). Significantly, the WISE centroids at W3 bands are substantially displaced from star B’s position by ∼2.8​″\sim 2.8\arcsec, but align better with the 1367.5 MHz radio source location by only ∼0.5​″\sim 0.5\arcsec (Figure 3). In the W4 band with lower resolution than W3, the centroid is almost 4​″4\arcsec away from star B, while only ∼1​″\sim 1\arcsec from the 1367.5 MHz radio source.

3.2 Astrometric diagnostics for candidate C, D, E and F

.

We conducted the centroiding analysis for all four S24 candidate stars with no associated radio sources: C, D, E, and F. All W1 and W2 centroids for the four cases were obtained using SEP, while all W4 images were fitted with scipy. For the W3 band, candidates C, D, and E were successfully fitted with SEP, but candidate F is elongated and could not be fitted by SEP, so we used scipy for its centroid. The astrometric results for these candidates are shown in Figure 4 and Table 5.

Candidate C (Gaia DR3 4649396037451459712) exhibits a positional offset between its W1&W2 emissions and W3&W4 emissions. As shown in Figure 4, the W3 and W4 emission centroids of candidate C are offset by ∼3.7​″\sim 3.7\arcsec and ∼5.0​″\sim 5.0\arcsec, respectively, from the propagated Gaia position. In contrast, the W1 and W2 centroid offsets from the propagated Gaia position are both <1​″<1\arcsec.

For candidates D, E, and F, their W1 and W2 centroid-to-stellar offsets are all below 1​″1\arcsec. Their W3 and W4 positional offsets are also less significant than those of C. Candidates D (Gaia DR3 2660349163149053824) and F (Gaia DR3 2956570141274256512) show relatively small stellar-to-centroid offsets in the W3 and W4 bands: their W3 centroids are all displaced by only ∼0.7​″\sim 0.7\arcsec, whereas the W4 centroids are by ∼1.8​″\sim 1.8\arcsec (Table 5). However, the W4 detections for candidates D and F have only 3–– 5σ\sigma significance above extended background structures, making the image quality insufficient to determine the true positional offsets in the W4 bands.

In contrast, candidate E (Gaia DR3 3190232820489766656) shows a larger centroid–stellar offset in both W3 and W4, with the emission centroids lying ∼1.5​″\sim 1.5\arcsec north of the star. The W3 and W4 centroids themselves differ by ∼1​″\sim 1\arcsec, and the detections are significant at >10​σ>10\sigma and >6​σ>6\sigma, respectively. The above results for candidate E make its ALLWISE positional offset more reliable than those of candidates D and F, and less susceptible to low-SNR artefacts.

3.3 Astrometric diagnostics for candidate H, I and J

We present the astrometric analysis of the three new Dyson Sphere candidates in K26. None of these sources shows associated radio or X-ray sources based on our VizieR cross-match. The corresponding ALLWISE images and centroid measurements are shown in Figure 5 and Table 6.

In the W1 and W2 bands, three candidates were detected using SEP, and their MIR centroids show offsets below 1​″1\arcsec. In the W3 and W4 bands, however, the MIR centroids—except for the W3 centroid of candidate H—were measured with scipy, as the image quality in these bands is too poor for reliable SEP detections. The W3 and W4 SNRs of candidates H, I, and J are generally low; only the W3 image of candidate J reaches a detection significance above 5​σ5\sigma.

Candidate H (Gaia DR3 2437221214075471744) shows a W3 centroid displaced by ∼2.6​″\sim 2.6\arcsec from the propagated Gaia position of the star with a ∼2​σ\sim 2\sigma detection. In the W4 band, the source appears elongated, showing a centroid-to-stellar offset of ∼1​″\sim 1\arcsec. The detailed spatial structure of this elongated source in the W4 is obscured due to the low image quality.

Similarly, the W3 and W4 images of candidates I and J exhibit elongated brightness profiles. Candidate I (Gaia DR3 3854090071297359616) appears as an extended stripe in W3, possibly arising from blended emission associated with multiple nearby sources, and is detected at only ∼2​σ\sim 2\sigma. In W4, the source exhibits a smoother morphology with a significance of ∼3​σ\sim 3\sigma. For W3, we derived the centroid using the southeastern peak emission, which is closest to the propagated Gaia position of the candidate star I. The resulting W3 and W4 centroids lie more than 2​″2\arcsec and 3​″3\arcsec from the propagated Gaia position, respectively. These centroids are oriented in nearly opposite directions, differing by over 5​″5\arcsec. The W4 centroid is offset by ∼3.2​″\sim 3.2\arcsec from the star I, showing a pattern similar to that seen for candidate C.

Candidate J (Gaia DR3 651765552072217216) also shows an offset between the W3 and W4 centroids. Its centroid-to-stellar offsets are ∼2​″\sim 2\arcsec in W3 and ∼6​″\sim 6\arcsec in W4, with opposite directions—the W3 and W4 centroids of candidate J are separated by more than 7​″7\arcsec, appearing as if W3 and W4 are dominated by two different MIR sources.

3.4 Comparison with the Photocentre for the S24 Candidate Stars

Candidates A and B in Subsection 3.1 and C, D, E, and F in Subsection 3.2 were analysed for photocentre offsets in S24. Although our centroiding methods are not identical to those used in S24, our results are broadly consistent with the values presented in Table 7 of S24. Our calculated W1/W2 total offsets remain below 0.5​″0.5\arcsec. For candidates B and C, the W1/W3 total offsets of 2.43​″2.43\arcsec and 3.64​″3.64\arcsec align with the significant displacements reported for those cases in S24. Table 7 presents a comparison between the results from S24 and this work.

For the remaining candidates (A, D, E, and F), W1/W3 positional offsets range from 0.7​″0.7\arcsec to 1.2​″1.2\arcsec. These do not deviate from the broad distributions suggested in Theissen and West (2017) (σ≈5​″\sigma\approx 5\arcsec), where band-to-band centroiding was applied to investigate warm dust around M dwarfs. While utilising precise Gaia DR3 positions in this work provides a better stellar reference, it does not inherently mitigate the degeneracy caused by such wide statistical ranges. To better examine the potential background contaminants of the candidate stars, we selected sets of SNR-matched control stars for a comparative analysis, as detailed in Section 3.5.

3.5 ALLWISE Image Centroid Analysis of Ordinary M-Dwarf Control Stars

To evaluate the statistical significance of our astrometric offsets, we constructed nine distinct control groups—one corresponding to each of the investigated candidates from A to J, except for candidate G, as it was previously identified as a clear case of AGN contamination (Ren, Garrett, and Siemion, 2025).

We first construct an initial parent sample of ordinary M-dwarfs by selecting sources from Gaia DR3 with a Renormalised Unit Weight Error (RUWE\mathrm{RUWE}) <1.4<1.4 located within 300​ pc300\text{~pc}. To specifically isolate the M-dwarf population, we applied multiple astrophysical constraints, including an absolute magnitude cut of MG>7.7M_{G}>7.7, an effective temperature threshold of Teff<4000​ KT_{\mathrm{eff}}<4000\text{~K}, and a colour cut of GBP−GRP≥1.84G_{\mathrm{BP}}-G_{\mathrm{RP}}\geq 1.84 (Montalto et al., 2021). Following cross-matching with the 2MASS and AllWISE catalogues, this initial selection yielded approximately 2 million sources.

Subsequently, we applied a series of stringent AllWISE photometric quality filters to ensure the reliability of the MIR detections. We required a signal-to-noise ratio SNR>3\mathrm{SNR}>3 in both the W3 and W4 bands, a criterion that excluded over 99 per cent of the sources. Additionally, we imposed profile-fit χ2\chi^{2} limits (w3rchi2<1.05\mathrm{w3rchi2}<1.05 and w4rchi2<1.3\mathrm{w4rchi2}<1.3), mandated the absence of saturation, and required at least one valid profile-fit measurement in both the W3 and W4 bands. Collectively, these requirements reduced the parent population to a final baseline sample of 3,582 high-quality ordinary M-dwarfs (see Table 8).

To establish a reliable astrometric baseline, we matched stars based on their Gaia DR3 GG-band magnitudes, W1 SNR, and W2 SNR. This selection relies on the premise that at these shorter wavelengths, corresponding to the Rayleigh–Jeans tail, the SEDs of potential Dyson Sphere candidates should remain similar from ordinary M-dwarfs. This matching criterion initially yielded 406 stars selected from the parent sample of 3,582. We then performed a direct visual inspection to ensure clear profile structures in the W3 and W4 bands, filtering the sample down to 155 final control stars across the nine groups (see Table 9).

We then performed a centroid analysis on the 155 stars, following the same procedure introduced in Section 2 and applied in Subsections 3.1, 3.2, and 3.3. For each star where a centroid was successfully detected, the centroiding process yielded both a positional offset and the corresponding uncertainty. The positional offsets of the control stars, used as the reference for the candidate stars, are then quantitatively analysed using the Rayleigh distribution by employing scipy.stats.rayleigh, with the quantitative results presented in Table 10.

The typical offset scale of the M dwarfs in each band of each control sample is characterised by the Rayleigh scale parameter σR\mathrm{\sigma_{R}}. Each candidate’s offset rcand\mathrm{r_{cand}} is compared against this scale to determine a Rayleigh survival probability pR\mathrm{p_{R}}, which represents the chance of a control star showing an offset at least as large. We then recast this probability as a one-sided Gaussian-equivalent significance, Φ−1​(1−pR)\Phi^{-1}(1-\mathrm{p_{R}}), which enables the standard-deviation expression. For all control samples, we also calculate an outlier-insensitive median scale σmed\mathrm{\sigma_{med}} as an independent baseline. This serves as a reliable backup because some datasets, most notably the W3 and W4 bands, do not strictly follow a Rayleigh distribution. As shown in Table 10, several cases (the W1 and W2 bands of C, and the W2 band of F) that are not significant under the Rayleigh scale reach >2​σ>2\sigma Gaussian-equivalent significance under the median scale. Appendix A presents further details of the Rayleigh-distribution analysis beyond the table, including the full centroid-offset distributions for all candidates and bands and an example of the control-star centroiding for candidate A.

Candidate A is the clear standout of the quantitative analysis in Table 10. In both its W1 and W2 bands, the Rayleigh fits yield pR<1×10−10\mathrm{p_{R}}<1\times 10^{-10} and a Gaussian-equivalent significance of >6​σ>6\sigma, indicating that its W1/W2 WISE centroids deviate strongly from clean M-dwarf behaviour. Whether this reflects an unrecognised background galaxy, an unusual debris configuration, or a genuine waste heat technosignature, the centroid profile of A is statistically distinct from the normal M-dwarf population in W1 and W2.

Among the other candidates, the next most significant after A are the W2 band of B (Gaussian-equivalent significance 3.7​σ3.7\sigma) and the W1 band of E (3.3​σ3.3\sigma). A marginal case is the W1 band of candidate B, with a Gaussian-equivalent significance of 2.3​σ2.3\sigma; here the median-scale baseline suggests a higher value of 4.3​σ4.3\sigma, indicating an anomalous offset discrepancy between candidate B and its control stars in the W1 band offsets. Similarly, the W1 and W2 bands of C and the W2 band of F show no significant deviation under the Rayleigh scale but reach Gaussian-equivalent significances of 3.1​σ3.1\sigma, 2.6​σ2.6\sigma and 2.7​σ2.7\sigma under the median scale, respectively (Table 10). However, across all bands, the centroid-to-stellar offsets for candidates D, H, I, and J do not behave as anomalies under either of the two quantitative analysis methods.

4.1 Searching for the AGN associated with Candidate G in the Optical and NIR regimes

To validate the effectiveness of detecting background contaminants using deep archival imaging, we first apply our analysis methodology to the hot DOG associated with candidate G (J2335–0004). This object was recently confirmed as a radio-loud AGN by Ren, Garrett, and Siemion (2025) using VLBI astrometry, and the ALLWISE positional offset analysis supports its hot DOG properties. J2335-0004 was effectively detected by the DESI Legacy Survey. The Legacy DR10 Tractor catalogue suggested that this object has an REX profile, consistent with an extended source (a galaxy). 5 Discussion

5.1 Evaluating the Likelihood of Contamination for the Nine Candidates

Building on the multi-wavelength and astrometric diagnostics presented in Sections 3 and 4, we can now classify the nine Dyson Sphere star candidates based on the likelihood of extragalactic contamination. Similar to the analysis of candidate G, we integrate these archival findings to identify which sources show clear signs of background contaminants and which remain ambiguous.

5.1.1 Candidates B and C: the most likely contaminated cases

Candidates B and C represent the two cases exhibiting the strongest evidence of background contaminants contributing to the MIR excess. For these sources, the archival data reveal the presence of background objects consistent with the observed infrared anomalies. Compared to the control-sample M dwarfs of similar GG-band magnitude and W​1W1/W​2W2 SNR, the two candidates exhibit significantly larger W1 and W2 bands emission centroid offsets from the propagated Gaia position, with a Gaussian-equivalent significance of >2​σ>2\sigma (Table 10).

As shown in Figures 3 and 7, the 1367.5 MHz and 1655.5 MHz radio sources of candidate B, along with the W3 and W4 centroids, are all spatially associated with the faint, extended tail/bulge structure visible in the Legacy Survey images, lying at the fringe of Candidate B’s PSF brightness profile. The spatial alignment, reinforced by the typical AGN spectral index of α∼0.59\alpha\sim 0.59 presented in Subsection 3.1, supports the presence of a background extragalactic source blended with the foreground candidate star B. The WISE emission maps (Figure 3) suggest that this background galaxy is likely a Hot DOG, which dominates the observed W3 and W4 bands MIR fluxes. From the view of WISE’s resolution, the 3​″3\arcsec galaxy-to-stellar offset makes the illusion that candidate star B itself is emitting the W3 and W4 excess fluxes, consistent with the predicted MIR signature of a modelled Dyson Sphere star.

Although candidate C lacks radio counterparts, its W3 and W4 emissions are offset from the central star by several arcseconds, similar to candidates B and G. The W3 and W4 centroids are spatially coincident with VMC J045603.25-741010.66, a source multiply detected in the VMC and Legacy surveys. Given that its location is nearly identical to the W3 centroid measured in our work, and there is no other nearby object detected, VMC J045603.25-741010.66 is likely the genuine contributor to the W3 and W4 band MIR flux excesses of candidate C. The WISE photometric characteristics of VMC J045603.25-741010.66 suggest that it is a potential hot DOG previously unknown to the literature. However, it appears undetected in both the RACS and GLEAM radio surveys. Frey et al. (2016). Such a non-detection is consistent with the typically radio-quiet nature of the Hot DOG population (Bridge et al., 2013). We further discuss the implications of this result and evaluate the expected radio-loud fraction of hot DOGs based on a sample of 144 confirmed hot DOGs in Section 5.2.

5.1.2 Candidates A: a suggestive contaminated case

Candidate A represents a highly suggestive case of contamination by a background Active Galactic Nucleus (AGN). Unlike candidates B and C, which have catalogued companions spatially separated from the propagated Gaia positions, candidate A’s radio companion (RACS-DR1 J124512.7-265206) is blended with the star position and thus not distinct. The radio spectral index of RACS-DR1 J124512.7-265206 (α∼0.39\alpha\sim 0.39) is characteristic of a flat-spectrum AGN. The radio sources, WISE centroids, and the propagated Gaia positions of the candidate stars are highly coincident, with mutual offsets of less than 2.5​″2.5\arcsec. As shown in Figure 7, these positions align closely within the brightness profile of the candidate star, and archival optical/NIR imaging reveals no other nearby sources. Given the statistical rarity of a flat-spectrum radio source aligning so precisely with a foreground star, it is highly probable that the star’s emission is being contaminated by a background AGN along the same line of sight. This alignment explains the minimal positional offsets observed across the different wavebands.

A further, independent line of evidence for this hidden AGN comes from the larger AllWISE centroid-to-stellar positional offsets compared to those of the control stars. In the Rayleigh significance analysis presented in Section 3.5, the offset between the Gaia position of candidate A and the WISE centroids yields the highest Gaussian-equivalent significance in both the W1 and W2 bands (Table 10), which offer the highest spatial resolution for characterising the stellar flux. That said, direct evidence is still lacking without dedicated high-resolution observations. Upcoming high-resolution observations from the JWST GO programme 7199 (Zackrisson et al., 2025) using the MIRI instrument will be essential to disentangle the mid-infrared emission from the stellar profile.

5.1.3 Candidates E, F, H and J: the candidates with ambiguous evidence

Candidates E and F present more ambiguous evidence than candidates A, B, and C, as they exhibit lower MIR centroid-to-stellar offsets and have no detected companion objects near the stellar brightness profile fringe that resemble extragalactic sources. While candidates A, B, and C exhibit W3 offsets exceeding 2​″2\arcsec, they also show Gaussian-equivalent significances of at least 2​σ2\sigma in both W1 and W2, derived from fitting a Rayleigh distribution to the control-star centroid offsets. By contrast, candidates E and F display >2​σ>2\sigma significance in only a single W1 or W2 band (Table 10), and their W3 offsets are also smaller. Specifically, candidate E shows a ∼3.3​σ\sim 3.3\sigma significance in the W1 band under a Rayleigh distribution scale (rather than a median scale), whereas candidate F exhibits a 2.7​σ2.7\sigma significance in the W2 band under the median scale. The W3 offsets for both candidates E and F remain below 1.5​″1.5\arcsec (Table 5).

The inspection of the images in Subsection 4.2 provides supplementary evaluation for candidates E and F. For candidate E, the closest external object is a galaxy-like source (VHS J040208.07-105434.4) located >5​″>5\arcsec away, which is too distant to interfere with the stellar MIR flux (Figure 8). Similarly, for candidate F, the nearest catalogued source—a PSF-profile object in the Legacy DR10 Tractor—is located over 5​″5\arcsec to the north. Additional faint objects near candidate F, including a southern EXP-profile source (Legacy DR8 ID: 8000189778005482) and several northeastern sources, also lie well outside the main MIR emission region.

Given that the visible neighbouring sources identified for candidates E and F are situated too distant to plausibly influence the MIR photometry, the >2​σ>2\sigma Gaussian-equivalent centroid offsets significance, which appear in the W2 band for E, and the W1 band for F, serve as indirect evidence of background contamination. The W1 and W2 bands are capable of tracing the stellar photosphere with high precision; therefore, significant offsets in these bands provide a reliable indicator of an underlying, unresolved source, even if the W3 flux is expected to be more strongly dominated by emission from a putative interloper. Although the overall evidence strength for E and F is lower than that of A, B, and C, the lack of spatially coincident background sources in deep survey imaging and photometric data suggests that while contaminants for E and F are likely present, the sources must be highly blended with the target stars.

Candidate H represents another ambiguous case. Its W3-band centroid is offset by ∼3.5​″\sim 3.5\arcsec from the propagated Gaia position and exceeds the W3-band offsets of candidates B and C. This is the highest W3-band offset and the only one exceeding 3​″3\arcsec among all nine candidates throughout this paper, while its W1 and W2 band offsets are only ∼0.5​″\sim 0.5\arcsec. Although the quantitative Rayleigh test in Subsection 3.5 suggests that the W3 offset does not deviate significantly from the control star distribution, the control sample contains only five stars. Therefore, this statistical result should not obscure the reality of the high positional W3-band offset of candidate H, which is clearly visible in Figure 5. Although its W4 centroid offset is smaller (<2.5​″<2.5\arcsec), the spatial MIR emission morphology appears elongated.

Using Legacy Survey data, we identified a faint object with a PSF brightness profile—likely a background galaxy (LegacyID: 10995408501213714)—situated to the northeast of candidate star H that contributes to the W4 flux (Figure 8). The blending of these two sources may result in the observed elongation of the W4 emission region. However, the SNR for the W3 and W4 emissions of candidate H is low (∼3​σ\sim 3\sigma), significantly undermining the reliability of its measured offsets. Furthermore, unlike candidate C, where the catalogued background galaxy is nearly coincident with the W3-band centroid, the catalogued background galaxy near candidate H lies ∼3​″\sim 3\arcsec from the W3 centroid.

The final ambiguous case is candidate J. As detailed in Section 4.4, we retrieved photometric evidence for an uncatalogued companion source at the stellar fringe, detected consistently in the Legacy gg and rr bands and tentatively in the UHS HH band, and exhibiting a point-source morphology. This companion is not, however, the only source projected onto the W3 emission of candidate J: it lies 3.35​″3.35\arcsec from the W3 centroid and 3.94​″3.94\arcsec from the W4 centroid, while the Legacy DR10 Tractor catalogue separately lists a resolved, galaxy-like source with an exponential (EXP) profile (Legacy ID 10995551421076405; Figure 13). As with candidate H, both putative contributors to the MIR flux are offset from the W3 centroid by ∼3​″\sim 3\arcsec rather than being coincident with it.

Since the candidate star J, the companion, and the catalogued galaxy-like source (Legacy ID: 10995551421076405) all lie within the W3 >5​σ>5\sigma emission region, the W3 flux cannot be unwarrantably attributed to a single source: owing to the blending and co-location within the WISE beam, it may plausibly be dominated by the star, by the companion, by the galaxy-like source, or by a combination of these objects. The W4 emission, by contrast, exhibits a significant positional offset from both the candidate star and the catalogued galaxy-like source, leaving the companion as the only optical/NIR feature that could be preliminarily identified as a contributor to the W4 flux. We accordingly regard candidate J as an ambiguous case of background contamination; unlike candidates A and B, whose blended companion can be identified as an AGN through its radio spectral index, the companion of candidate J lacks comparably direct evidence for its nature, and definitively assigning the mid-infrared emission to any single feature will require future observations of higher angular resolution and sensitivity. Given the lack of exact spatial coincidence between the identified galaxies and the W3 centroids, both candidates H and J carry significantly lower confidence than candidates A, B and C. Consequently, we relegate both candidates H and J to a lower, ambiguous classification tier.

In summary, while the specific evidence for candidates E, F, H, and J is more ambiguous than for A, B, and C, owing variously to low SNR, blending, or the lack of spatially coincident background sources, their significant positional offsets still indicatively suggest background contamination. Resolving these cases requires dedicated higher-resolution observations, such as the approved observations of candidate E via the JWST GO programme 7199 (Zackrisson et al., 2025).

5.1.4 Candidates that lack critical evidence for MIR contamination

Candidates D and I lack both clear evidence of contamination and the definitive proof needed for a final classification. Without radio detections, their analysis relies solely on ALLWISE centroids and archival optical/NIR imaging analysis, following the same methodology used for candidates C, E, F, H, and J.

Candidate D presents a spatial contradiction: while Legacy imaging reveals an REX-profile galaxy (Legacy ID: 10995494344987809) on the southeastern fringe of the stellar profile (Figure 11), the WISE centroids are oriented toward the north. The MIR imaging shows no clear spatial separation between the WISE MIR spatial emission and the position of candidate star D (Figure 4). As detailed in Subsection 3.4, candidate D exhibits the smallest positional offset among all the candidates. Furthermore, the four-band comparison with the control star offsets presented in Subsection 3.5 demonstrates that none of the WISE bands for candidate D pass the Rayleigh distribution test, suggesting that its offsets are statistically indistinguishable from the control population. A plausible explanation for the low-level excess is a proximate, highly blended AGN—an arrangement analogous to the blending of candidate A, but without radio emission to confirm an extragalactic nature. Despite candidate D lying within the footprint of multiple major radio surveys (δ≈+05∘\delta\approx+05^{\circ}), the absence of a radio counterpart suggests that any contaminant would have to be radio-quiet. Ultimately, the true origin of this emission will be determined by high-resolution MIRI data from the JWST GO 7199 programme (Zackrisson et al., 2025).

The final case is candidate I. Its W3 centroid appears to be located on the fringe of the stellar brightness profile, while the W4 centroid is positioned outside the profile. The W3 emission, however, displays a stripe-like structure with only ∼2​σ\sim 2\sigma significance, making it ambiguous for reliably indicating the true background source position. Its W4 emission, with >3​σ>3\sigma significance, shows an offset of ∼3.2​″\sim 3.2\arcsec from the propagated Gaia position. However, the comparison in Subsection 3.5 does not support the presence of a significant offset discrepancy relative to the control stars. Unlike candidates H and J, for which deep optical/NIR imaging revealed sources projected onto the high W3 and/or W4 flux regions, we find no such counterpart for the MIR emission of candidate I in the deep Legacy Survey and UHS images. Candidate I has neither a statistically robust positional offset, unlike candidates E and F, nor an identifiable optical/NIR counterpart, unlike candidates H and J. It therefore presents the weakest evidence among the candidates. We accordingly place it in the lowest classification class, along with candidate D. If a faint background AGN spatially coincident with the stellar profile is the genuine MIR contributor, high-resolution imaging will be required to disentangle these components, particularly since candidate I was not included in the JWST GO 7199 programme.

5.2 The Radio Detection Rate of Hot Dust-Obscured Galaxies

Among the ten Dyson Sphere candidates analysed in this work, only three (A, B, and G) were detected in archival radio surveys. If one assumes that all ten candidates are contaminated by Hot Dust-Obscured Galaxies (Hot DOGs), the remaining seven would necessarily be radio-quiet AGNs. To evaluate the plausibility of this scenario, we examined a sample of 144 catalogued Hot DOGs from Assef et al. (2015)—a robust sample selected from WISE data and verified with ground-based NIR observations.

We queried the VizieR service for radio counterparts within this reference sample and identified only 12 detections (Table 13), representing a detection rate of <10%<10\%. The radio catalogues include: the FIRST survey(Becker et al. 1995), NVSS (Condon et al. 1998), SUMSS (Mauch et al. 2003), the TIFR GMRT Sky Survey (TGSS; Intema et al. 2017), the Australia Telescope Compact Array XXL survey (ATCA XXL; Butler et al. 2018), the VLA Sky Survey (VLASS; Lacy et al. 2020), the RACS surveys (Hale et al. 2021; Duchesne et al. 2024, 2025), the LOFAR Two-metre Sky Survey Data Release 2 (LoTSS DR2; Shimwell et al. 2022), and the GLEAM-X survey (Ross et al. 2024). This low detection rate is consistent with the characterisation of similar WISE-selected dusty extragalactic sources as typically radio-quiet (Bridge et al., 2013). Consequently, the lack of radio emission in seven out of ten candidates aligns with the low radio-loud fraction expected for this galaxy class.

6 Conclusions

We conducted a combined analysis of nine Project Hephaistos Dyson Sphere candidates using two complementary approaches based on archival data: (1) an astrometric examination of AllWISE four-band image centroids to identify mid-infrared (MIR) flux offsets relative to the propagated Gaia positions, a method whose effectiveness can be validated through a comparative analysis of ordinary M-dwarf control samples, and (2) a search for potential extragalactic companions in deep NIR and optical images.

By comparing the WISE centroids with the Gaia stellar positions propagated to the AllWISE epoch (the first method), we found that candidates B and C exhibit the largest positional offsets, with W3 centroid-to-stellar offsets of approximately 3​″3\arcsec. Notably, the W3 centroid of candidate B aligns with RACS 1367.5 MHz and 1655.5 MHz radio counterparts to within <1​″<1\arcsec, indicating that a radio source dominates the emission observed in the W3 and W4 bands.

Using the second method—searching for extragalactic companions in deep imaging, we confirmed that J2335-0004, the Hot DOG which contaminated candidate G, is present and catalogued in the VISTA and Legacy surveys. Although no radio companion was found near candidate C, we found a faint NIR source located at the position of the W3 centroid in the VMC data. These results suggest that both B and C are likely Hot DOGs, similar to the MIR positional offset observed in candidate G, which was later confirmed as a hot DOG by high-resolution radio observations (Ren, Garrett, and Siemion, 2025).

We also identified candidate A as a suggestive case. It exhibits a radio counterpart with an AGN-like spectral index. Because its centroid-to-stellar offset is far larger than those of the control sample stars, the close proximity between the propagated Gaia positions of the star, radio source, and WISE centroids implies that the contaminating background source must lie nearly along the line of sight.

Candidates E, F, H and J are classified as a lower, ambiguous class: marginal cases requiring further evidence to assess the potential contamination. For E and F, although their MIR centroid-to-stellar offsets reach a Gaussian-equivalent significance above 2​σ2\sigma in one of the four WISE bands, the remaining bands show no offset excess, and no counterparts can be confirmed in deep imaging to serve as a clear source of contamination. Candidate H displays the largest W3-band offset among the nine candidates, and candidate J exhibits multiple likely background contaminants; for both, some possible contaminating sources were identified nearby, but low SNR or blending limits the reliability of these associations.

Lastly, candidates D and I show no radio counterparts, no unambiguously outstanding centroid offsets, and no faint companions that could plausibly contribute to the MIR excess. However, we suspect that these sources might also be contaminated by radio-quiet AGNs that lie close to the line of sight towards the M-dwarf star. Our identification of a sample of 144 Hot DOGs suggests that more than 90% of them are radio-quiet. This result agrees with the low radio-detection rate observed in the Project Hephaistos Dyson Sphere candidate sample.

Despite the resolution limitations of current MIR datasets like the WISE archive, our analysis demonstrates that supplementary multi-wavelength data offer valuable insights into the nature of these Dyson Sphere candidates. The suspected contaminants near Candidates B and C still require confirmation of their extragalactic nature. Dedicated observations, such as deep infrared imaging and spectral analysis, are essential to conclusively disentangle hypothetical Hot DOGs from the foreground stars. Such observations are equally critical for resolving the possible cases (A, E, F, H, and J) and clarifying the nature of candidates D and I, where radio data remains insufficient. In this regard, the ongoing JWST GO 7199 observations of Candidates A, D, and E will provide the direct results needed to identify the genuine contributors to their infrared excess. Ultimately, rigorously identifying and eliminating such false positives is a fundamental step in the search for techno-signatures; by systematically ruling out natural contamination, we can ensure that future resources focus only on the most robust and compelling Dyson Sphere candidates.

Appendix A Centroid-offset distributions of the control samples

As described in Section 3.5, we performed the centroid analysis on the full set of 155 control stars, grouped into nine control samples corresponding to the nine candidates. To illustrate the procedure, Figure 15 shows the centroiding results for the 15 control stars of Candidate A as an example. For each star and WISE band, we mark the Gaia position propagated to the corresponding WISE observation epoch and the measured centroid, from which the positional offset and associated uncertainty are derived. During the positional analysis of the control stars, the Gaia astrometric data are used to propagate the stellar positions to the WISE observation epochs, accounting for proper motion and parallax.

To further address the centroiding analysis presented in Section 3.5, we provide the complete set of results for all targets in Fig. 16. This figure displays the full grid of centroid-offset distributions for the control samples of nine candidates across the four WISE bands. In each panel, a direct visual comparison can be made between the distribution of the background control stars and the specific offset of the candidate. This comprehensive visualisation directly reflects the statistical metrics compiled in Table 10; in particular, the significantly higher offsets of candidates A, B, C, E, and F relative to their baseline control populations are clearly apparent.
