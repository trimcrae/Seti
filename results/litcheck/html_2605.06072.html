Phase‑Space Crystallization in Galactic Globular Clusters: A Gaia‑Based Metric and Implications for Technosignature Searches

Bo-Lun Huang Institute for Frontiers in Astronomy and Astrophysics, Beijing Normal University, Beijing 102206, China School of Physics and Astronomy, Beijing Normal University, Beijing 100875, China Bolunh@hotmail.com Zhen-Zhao Tao College of Computer and Information Engineering, Dezhou University, Dezhou 253023, China tzzzxc@163.com Tong-Jie Zhang Institute for Frontiers in Astronomy and Astrophysics, Beijing Normal University, Beijing 102206, China School of Physics and Astronomy, Beijing Normal University, Beijing 100875, China tjzhang@bnu.edu.cn
Abstract

We develop a model-independent framework to quantify phase-space “crystallization”, the degree of ordered radial and kinematic substructure, in 79 Galactic globular clusters using the Gaia EDR3-based membership catalogue of Vasiliev and Baumgardt (2021a). We construct a scalar crystallization index, CindexC_{\rm index}, by combining a radial inhomogeneity metric (zradz_{\rm rad}) and a local, cluster-centric tangential-velocity metric (zvelz_{\rm vel}) standardized against empirical nulls. The population distribution is strongly non-Gaussian: most clusters are consistent with smooth, equilibrium expectations, while a small high-CC tail (Cindex≥2C_{\rm index}\geq 2) identifies dynamically complex systems, including NGC 5139 (ω\omega Cen) and NGC 104 (47 Tuc). Correlation and fixed-NN tests show that sample size affects detectability, but does not by itself explain all high-rank objects. Through synthetic injection tests in dynamically “quiet” control clusters, we demonstrate sensitivity to ultra-cold, shell-confined kinematic components, ruling out single-shell structures comprising more than a few to ∼10\sim 10–20% of core stars in the best-sampled control clusters. We find no evidence, within the sensitivity of the adopted diagnostics, for phase-space structures that require explanations beyond known dynamical processes. However, CindexC_{\rm index} provides a useful tool for ranking clusters by dynamical extremeness, serving both as a diagnostic for internal complexity and as a quantitative metric for prioritizing follow-up dynamical or technosignature-oriented observations.

I Introduction

Globular clusters (GCs) are among the oldest bound stellar systems in the Galaxy and remain key laboratories for stellar dynamics, stellar evolution, and hierarchical galaxy assembly (e.g. Meylan and Heggie, 1997; Baumgardt and Hilker, 2018). Their present–day phase–space structure encodes the integrated effects of two–body relaxation, mass segregation, tidal stripping, internal rotation, and (in some cases) complex formation histories. In the last decade, high–precision astrometry from the Gaia mission (Gaia Collaboration et al., 2016, 2021) has transformed this field: for many Milky Way GCs we now possess six–dimensional phase–space information and membership probabilities for thousands of stars per cluster (Vasiliev, 2019; Vasiliev and Baumgardt, 2021a; Baumgardt and Vasiliev, 2021). This enables systematic, comparative studies of GC kinematics and substructure at a level that was not previously possible.

Evidence is accumulating that some clusters exhibit rich internal phase–space phenomenology: rotation and velocity anisotropy, multiple chemo–kinematic populations, and tidal debris connecting the cluster to stellar streams in the Galactic halo (e.g. Ibata et al., 2019). In particular, very cold and coherent components, thin streams, shells, or kinematically narrow substructures, represent strong departures from the quasi–thermal, smooth distributions expected for dynamically old systems in steady tidal fields. Such structures can arise naturally from tidal stripping, mergers, or incomplete violent relaxation, but they are also precisely the kinds of features that would be produced by any process that re–arranges or selects stars in phase space in a highly correlated way. From the broader technosignature perspective, several authors have argued that artificial large–scale engineering, if it exists at all, is most plausibly sought via its dynamical or energetic footprints rather than by assuming a particular communication technology (e.g. Wright et al., 2014; Wright, 2018). In this context, unusually ordered or ”crystallized” phase–space structure in a dense, ancient stellar system is an intriguing target class to quantify, even if the default expectation is that any such signatures will be entirely natural in origin.

A number of non–parametric or weakly parametric kinematic diagnostics already probe departures from simple equilibrium, including higher–order moments of proper–motion distributions such as skewness and kurtosis (e.g. Heyl et al., 2017; Ziliotto et al., 2025, 2026). Our claim is therefore narrower: there is not yet a single survey–style scalar that jointly ranks the Milky Way GC population by radial graininess and local velocity-distribution shape using a common empirical null. Most detailed dynamical studies focus on individual clusters, rotation curves, anisotropy profiles, or population-resolved moment maps. What is missing for our purpose is a homogeneous diagnostic that: (1) can be computed for every cluster with sufficient Gaia membership statistics; (2) is sensitive to small–scale radial and kinematic substructure; and (3) is calibrated against an explicit null hypothesis representing the expected level of “graininess” from finite–NN sampling and measurement errors alone.

In this work we construct such a diagnostic, which we refer to as a crystallization index for Galactic globular clusters. Starting from the membership catalogue of Vasiliev and Baumgardt (2021a) and the associated cluster parameter compilation of Baumgardt and Vasiliev (2021), we build star–by–star samples for 79 Milky Way GCs with robust phase–space information. For each cluster we define: (i) a radial structure metric QradQ_{\rm rad} that quantifies deviations of the normalized projected radius distribution from a locally smoothed reference profile; and (ii) a local tangential velocity metric Qvel,localQ_{\rm vel,local} that compares the observed distribution of tangential speeds within radial shells to a Rayleigh null model calibrated on the full cluster ensemble. These ingredients are then combined into a single, dimensionless crystallization index CindexC_{\rm index}, constructed from zz–scores relative to null or ensemble distributions, which ranks clusters by the joint strength of their radial and kinematic substructure.

A key element of our approach is that the null model for the radial structure metric is empirical rather than purely analytic. We generate ”scrambled” or phase–randomized realizations of each cluster that preserve its radial selection function, membership statistics, and basic scaling properties, but erase any coherent higher–order structure. By processing these null realizations through the same QradQ_{\rm rad} metric, we obtain, for each cluster, a baseline expectation and scatter arising purely from shot noise and the cluster’s mean profile. For the kinematic metric we adopt an ensemble–based normalization in which the distribution of Qvel,localQ_{\rm vel,local} values across the 79 GCs serves as an empirical reference. This allows us to express the observed metrics of each cluster as standardized deviations (zrad,zvel)(z_{\rm rad},z_{\rm vel}) and to define CindexC_{\rm index} in a way that is explicitly corrected, at least in a first approximation, for the sample size, completeness, and heterogeneity of the input catalogs.

We then use CindexC_{\rm index} to partition the GC population into four classification tiers. Tier 1 clusters (high CindexC_{\rm index}) are those whose combined radial and kinematic structure is most inconsistent with the null or ensemble baseline, and thus are highest priority for detailed dynamical follow–up; this ranking by itself is not evidence for artificial structure, and natural explanations are expected to dominate. Tier 2 and 3 clusters occupy an intermediate regime, while Tier 4 clusters define a ”control” sample with Cindex≈0C_{\rm index}\approx 0 that behaves, within uncertainties, like phase–scrambled realizations. Finally, we perform controlled injection tests in several Tier 4 clusters, adding artificial ultra–cold components of varying strength and radial extent to quantify the sensitivity of CindexC_{\rm index} to embedded kinematic substructures and to translate non–detections into quantitative upper limits on a specific class of extremely cold, single–shell phase–space structures.

The goals of this paper are therefore twofold. First, we introduce and justify a practical crystallization metric CindexC_{\rm index} for Galactic GCs, together with its radial and velocity components, and we characterize its behaviour across the current Gaia–era GC sample. Second, we demonstrate how this framework can be used both for conventional dynamical applications (e.g. identifying clusters with unusually structured phase space) and for placing preliminary constraints on any hypothetical processes that would generate extremely cold, coherent kinematic signatures in dense stellar systems. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section II we describe the input catalogues, membership selections, and derived quantities used in our analysis. Section III defines the QradQ_{\rm rad} and Qvel,localQ_{\rm vel,local} metrics and the construction of the crystallization index. Section IV presents the resulting distribution of CindexC_{\rm index} across the GC population and our tier classification. In Section V we describe the injection experiments and derive quantitative sensitivity limits for several representative clusters. We discuss the implications, limitations, and possible extensions of this framework in Section VI, and summarize our conclusions in Section VII.

II Data and Cluster Sample

II.1 Cluster parameter catalogue

Our analysis is based on the homogeneous compilation of Milky Way globular cluster (GC) parameters presented by Baumgardt and Vasiliev (2021), which combines Gaia EDR3 astrometry with HST and ground–based photometry to obtain distances, structural parameters, and orbital information for a large, homogeneous subset of the known Galactic GC system. For each cluster we take from their catalogue: the heliocentric distance R⊙R_{\odot}, Galactocentric distance RGCR_{\rm GC}, projected half–light radius rhr_{h} in arcminutes and parsecs, total cluster mass MclM_{\rm cl} and its logarithm log⁡Mcl\log M_{\rm cl}, and a set of orbital parameters including pericentric distance RPerR_{\rm Per} and a coarse family classification based on orbital characteristics (their “family_id” flag).

Starting from the Baumgardt and Vasiliev (2021) sample, we restrict attention to clusters for which the Vasiliev and Baumgardt (2021a) membership catalogue (Section II.2) provides sufficiently large and clean samples of member stars with Gaia EDR3 astrometry. Specifically, the fiducial analysis requires: (1) the existence of a published membership solution; (2) Pmem≥0.9P_{\rm mem}\geq 0.9; (3) 0.5≤Rnorm≤3.00.5\leq R_{\rm norm}\leq 3.0; (4) a magnitude cut retaining stars brighter than the 90th percentile in GG within the radial window; and (5) a final analyzed sample size Ncore≥700N_{\rm core}\geq 700. Applying these criteria yields a working sample of 79 Galactic GCs; the NcoreN_{\rm core} values reported in Tables 2 and 3 are therefore the final numbers of stars entering the crystallization metrics, not pre-selection catalogue counts.

II.2 Membership catalogue and core sample

Stellar memberships are taken from the Vasiliev and Baumgardt (2021a) catalogue, which provides probabilistic Gaia EDR3–based memberships for stars in the vicinity of 170 Milky Way GCs. This catalogue is built by jointly modelling the spatial, proper–motion, and parallax distributions of cluster and field stars using mixture models, yielding, for each star, a membership probability PmemP_{\rm mem} along with revised cluster mean astrometric parameters. For each cluster we retrieve all stars with reported PmemP_{\rm mem}, together with their sky coordinates (α,δ)(\alpha,\delta), proper motions (μα∗,μδ)(\mu_{\alpha^{\ast}},\mu_{\delta}), parallaxes, and associated uncertainties. We retain the EDR3-based astrometry because the main astrometric solution in Gaia DR3 is the same as in EDR3 (Gaia Collaboration et al., 2023), while the published membership catalogue and its validation were constructed on the EDR3 solution; the persistent source_id values would allow future cross-matches to DR3 non-astrometric products. We also make use of the catalogue of variable-star memberships in Galactic globular clusters from the associated Zenodo data release (Vasiliev and Baumgardt, 2021b).

We construct our working “core” sample in several stages. First, we define a high–probability membership cut, adopting Pmem≥0.9P_{\rm mem}\geq 0.9 as our default threshold. This choice balances purity and completeness: it retains the bulk of the cluster population while strongly suppressing contamination from the Galactic field (see Vasiliev and Baumgardt, 2021a). Second, we require finite positions, proper motions, proper-motion uncertainties, and parallaxes as a guard against invalid entries; in practice the Vasiliev and Baumgardt (2021a) catalogue already excludes stars without usable proper motions, so this step is only a reproducibility safeguard. The public membership table used here does not include RUWE, and we therefore do not impose an additional RUWE cut in the fiducial analysis; the membership probability cut, the finite-astrometry requirement, and the magnitude cut described above are the quality cuts that define the analysis sample. Third, we assign each star a projected angular radius RarcminR_{\rm arcmin} from the cluster center given by Baumgardt and Vasiliev (2021), and convert this to a normalized radius

Rnorm≡Rarcminrh,arcmin,R_{\rm norm}\equiv\frac{R_{\rm arcmin}}{r_{h,{\rm arcmin}}}\,, (1)

where rh,arcminr_{h,{\rm arcmin}} is the projected half–light radius in arcminutes. Finally, for the purpose of defining our structure metrics we restrict to stars in the normalized radial range

0.5≤Rnorm≤ 3.0,0.5\;\leq\;R_{\rm norm}\;\leq\;3.0\,, (2)

which typically encompasses the outer core and inner halo. The lower bound avoids the very center, where crowding and incompleteness are most severe for Gaia, while the upper bound avoids the strongly field–contaminated outskirts and truncation region.

For the 79 clusters in our working sample, the fraction of high–probability members that fall into this 0.5≤Rnorm≤3.00.5\leq R_{\rm norm}\leq 3.0 window is substantial: the median fraction is fcore=0.68f_{\rm core}=0.68, with a 10–90% range of 0.510.51–0.770.77. Thus our metrics are sensitive to the majority of well–measured members while deliberately excluding the most problematic central and outermost regions.

The resulting core catalogue contains, for each star, its Gaia source_id, cluster identifier, (α,δ)(\alpha,\delta), (μα∗,μδ)(\mu_{\alpha^{\ast}},\mu_{\delta}) and their uncertainties, parallax, membership probability, GG magnitude, RarcminR_{\rm arcmin}, and RnormR_{\rm norm}. Hereafter NcoreN_{\rm core} denotes the number of stars that pass all fiducial cuts and are actually used in the structure metrics; it is the same quantity listed as NstarsN_{\rm stars} in Tables 2 and 3. By construction, this catalogue is free of obvious non–members and gross astrometric outliers, and provides the common starting point for both the radial and kinematic structure diagnostics described in Section III.

III Methods: Radial and Kinematic Structure Metrics

Our goal is to construct a dimensionless ”crystallization index” that ranks globular clusters by the strength of their small–scale phase–space structure, while accounting for finite–NN noise and different sample sizes. In this section we describe the construction of two intermediate metrics, a radial structure statistic QradQ_{\rm rad} and a local tangential–velocity statistic Qvel,localQ_{\rm vel,local}, and how they are combined into the final crystallization index CindexC_{\rm index} and classification tiers.

Throughout this work we use the notation RnormR_{\rm norm} for the projected angular radius RarcminR_{\rm arcmin} normalized by the projected half–light radius rh,arcminr_{h,{\rm arcmin}},

Rnorm≡Rarcminrh,arcmin,R_{\rm norm}\equiv\frac{R_{\rm arcmin}}{r_{h,{\rm arcmin}}}\,, (7)

as defined in Section II. Unless otherwise stated, all structure metrics are evaluated for stars in the normalized radial range

0.5≤Rnorm≤ 3.0,0.5\;\leq\;R_{\rm norm}\;\leq\;3.0\,, (8)

which typically encompasses the outer core and inner halo of each cluster.

III.1 Radial binning and normalized profiles

For each cluster, we begin by selecting all stars in the core sample (Section II.2) that satisfy Equation (8). We then construct a binned representation of the normalized radial profile by dividing the interval [Rnormmin,Rnormmax]=[0.5,3.0][R_{\rm norm}^{\rm min},R_{\rm norm}^{\rm max}]=[0.5,3.0] into KK equal–width bins in RnormR_{\rm norm}. In this work we adopt K=30K=30, which gives a mean occupancy of at least Ncore/K≃24N_{\rm core}/K\simeq 24 stars per bin even for the smallest analyzed clusters and substantially larger occupancies for most systems. Equal-number radial bins are useful for some density-profile applications, but they are not fiducial here because they would force the count vector toward uniformity and therefore suppress precisely the localized radial over- and under-densities that QradQ_{\rm rad} is designed to detect. Instead, we keep fixed radial scale in units of rhr_{h} and calibrate finite-count effects with the cluster-specific null model in Section III.3. We also verified robustness to coarser and finer equal-width choices (K=20K=20 and 4040); the identity of the main high-CC clusters is unchanged, although the radial-only amplitude of individual clusters varies as expected with scale.

Let {Ri}\{R_{i}\} denote the set of NN normalized radii for a given cluster after this selection. We define the bin edges

Rkedge=Rnormmin+k​Δ​R,k=0,1,…,K,R_{k}^{\rm edge}=R_{\rm norm}^{\rm min}+k\,\Delta R\,,\quad k=0,1,\ldots,K\,, (9)

with Δ​R=(Rnormmax−Rnormmin)/K\Delta R=(R_{\rm norm}^{\rm max}-R_{\rm norm}^{\rm min})/K. The observed counts in each bin are then

forming a discrete representation of the cluster’s radial distribution in normalized coordinates.

III.2 Radial structure metric

We wish to quantify, in a model–light way, how “lumpy” or structured the radial profile is on bin–to–bin scales, relative to a smooth baseline that follows the broad trend of the data. In this operational definition, “structured” means a statistically significant excess or deficit in the one-dimensional projected-radius counts on scales comparable to one or a few bins; it does not by itself identify the physical cause. Known multiple stellar populations with different radial concentrations, tidal distortions, patchy completeness, or residual field contamination could all contribute if they imprint sharp enough radial features within 0.5≤Rnorm≤30.5\leq R_{\rm norm}\leq 3. To this end we construct, from the observed counts {nk}\{n_{k}\}, a smoothed reference profile {λ^k}\{\hat{\lambda}_{k}\} using a three–bin moving average:

This construction preserves the global shape of the radial profile while suppressing small–scale fluctuations on the scale of a single bin. It can be interpreted as a data–driven estimate of the underlying ”smooth” surface–density profile, without imposing a specific parametric model such as a King or Plummer law.

For each cluster we record, in addition to QradQ_{\rm rad}, the effective number of contributing bins KeffK_{\rm eff} and summary statistics of the observed bin counts (minimum, maximum, mean, and standard deviation). These quantities are used as diagnostics to confirm that the chosen binning and radial range provide sufficient dynamic range across the sample.

III.3 Radial null model

The raw value of QradQ_{\rm rad} is not directly comparable between clusters with widely differing numbers of stars. Even in the absence of any genuine structure, finite–NN sampling causes QradQ_{\rm rad} to scatter around unity with a dispersion that depends on the total count NN and the distribution of stars across bins. To place clusters on a common footing we construct, for each cluster individually, a null ensemble of phase–scrambled realizations that represent the expected distribution of QradQ_{\rm rad} for a smooth system with the same mean profile and total number of stars.

The null construction proceeds as follows. For a given cluster we treat the smoothed profile {λ^k}\{\hat{\lambda}_{k}\} from Equation (11) as an estimate of the underlying radial density. We convert it into a discrete probability distribution

and generate NnullN_{\rm null} Monte Carlo realizations (we adopt Nnull=200N_{\rm null}=200 as our fiducial choice) in which NN stars are drawn independently from this distribution. Each realization yields a mock set of bin counts {nk(mock)}\{n_{k}^{\rm(mock)}\}, from which we recompute λ^k(mock)\hat{\lambda}_{k}^{\rm(mock)}, zk(mock)z_{k}^{\rm(mock)}, and Qrad(mock)Q_{\rm rad}^{\rm(mock)} using the same procedure as for the real data.

From the ensemble of mock Qrad(mock)Q_{\rm rad}^{\rm(mock)} values we measure a mean and standard deviation,

⟨Qrad⟩null,σQ,null≡std​(Qrad(mock)).\langle Q_{\rm rad}\rangle_{\rm null},\qquad\sigma_{Q,{\rm null}}\equiv{\rm std}\left(Q_{\rm rad}^{\rm(mock)}\right)\,. (15)

We then express the observed QradQ_{\rm rad} for that cluster as a standardized deviation from its own null ensemble,

zrad≡Qradobs−⟨Qrad⟩nullσQ,null.z_{\rm rad}\equiv\frac{Q_{\rm rad}^{\rm obs}-\langle Q_{\rm rad}\rangle_{\rm null}}{\sigma_{Q,{\rm null}}}\,. (16)

In what follows we adopt

zrad,pos≡max⁡(0,zrad)z_{\rm rad,pos}\equiv\max\left(0,\,z_{\rm rad}\right) (17)

as the radial component contributing to the crystallization index, since zrad<0z_{\rm rad}<0 is more naturally interpreted as an absence of structure rather than as evidence for ”anti–crystallization.”

III.4 Local tangential–velocity structure statistic

The radial metric QradQ_{\rm rad} captures inhomogeneities in the projected spatial distribution of stars. To probe kinematic crystallization we require a complementary metric that quantifies deviations of the tangential–velocity distribution from a smooth, isotropic reference, with particular sensitivity to cold substructures confined to restricted radial shells.

We operate on the tangential speeds vtanv_{\rm tan} defined in Section II. For each cluster we select stars in the normalized radial range 0.5≤Rnorm≤3.00.5\leq R_{\rm norm}\leq 3.0 and partition this interval into a small number of broader radial shells. In the main analysis we adopt three shells,

shell1:0.5≤Rnorm<1.0,shell2:1.0≤Rnorm<2.0,shell3:2.0≤Rnorm≤3.0,{\rm shell~1}:0.5\leq R_{\rm norm}<1.0,\quad{\rm shell~2}:1.0\leq R_{\rm norm}<2.0,\quad{\rm shell~3}:2.0\leq R_{\rm norm}\leq 3.0\,, (18)

rather than equal-number shells, because the fixed boundaries preserve a common physical meaning across clusters: inner high-crowding boundary region, intermediate half-light-radius region, and outer inner-halo region. Equal-number shells would mix these radial regimes differently from cluster to cluster. In the current sample shell 2 is also the best-populated of the three shells (median shell counts of 889, 1460, and 626 stars for shells 1–3, respectively), while avoiding both the most crowded inner region and the most contamination-prone outer boundary. For each shell we compute a shell–level statistic QsQ_{s} using the following procedure.

and compute the expected fraction of stars in each bin under a Rayleigh distribution with scale parameter 11. Writing FRayleigh​(x)F_{\rm Rayleigh}(x) for the cumulative distribution function, the expected bin probabilities are

pm=FRayleigh​(xm+1edge)−FRayleigh​(xmedge),p_{m}=F_{\rm Rayleigh}(x_{m+1}^{\rm edge})-F_{\rm Rayleigh}(x_{m}^{\rm edge})\,, (22)

and the corresponding expected counts are λm=Ns​pm\lambda_{m}=N_{s}\,p_{m}. We restrict attention to bins with λm≥λmin\lambda_{m}\geq\lambda_{\rm min} (typically λmin=1\lambda_{\rm min}=1) to avoid division by very small numbers. For these bins we define a per–bin chi–square–like contribution,

χm=(nm−λm)2λm,\chi_{m}=\frac{\left(n_{m}-\lambda_{m}\right)^{2}}{\lambda_{m}}\,, (23)

and then take the root–mean–square over the MeffM_{\rm eff} bins that pass the λm\lambda_{m} threshold,

Qs≡[1Meff​∑m:λm≥λminχm]1/2.Q_{s}\equiv\left[\frac{1}{M_{\rm eff}}\sum_{m:\,\lambda_{m}\geq\lambda_{\rm min}}\chi_{m}\right]^{1/2}. (24)

If the shell’s normalized velocity distribution is fully consistent with the Rayleigh null, then QsQ_{s} will be of order unity; localized excesses or deficits in one or a few bins (e.g. from a narrow cold component) will drive QsQ_{s} above unity.

To obtain a single local kinematic structure metric for each cluster, we combine the shell–level statistics QsQ_{s} into a star-count-weighted root-mean-square,

Qvel,local≡(∑sws​Qs2∑sws)1/2,Q_{\rm vel,local}\equiv\left(\frac{\sum_{s}w_{s}\,Q_{s}^{2}}{\sum_{s}w_{s}}\right)^{1/2}\,, (25)

with weights wsw_{s} proportional to the number of stars in each shell (we use ws=Nsw_{s}=N_{s}). The RMS form is the expression implemented in the analysis and gives more weight to a shell that is strongly non-Rayleigh than a simple arithmetic mean would. In practice, shell 2 (intermediate radii) carries the most weight in this combination, reflecting both its larger star counts and its role as the least crowded, least edge-contaminated region where embedded kinematic structures would be easiest to identify.

III.5 Kinematic normalization

Unlike the radial metric, where we construct a null ensemble for each cluster separately, for the local tangential–velocity metric we adopt an ensemble–based normalization. We compute Qvel,localQ_{\rm vel,local} for every cluster in the sample as described in Section III.4, and then work with its logarithm. Over the set of all clusters we measure

μlog⁡Q≡⟨log10⁡Qvel,local⟩,σlog⁡Q≡std​(log10⁡Qvel,local).\mu_{\log Q}\equiv\left\langle\log_{10}Q_{\rm vel,local}\right\rangle,\qquad\sigma_{\log Q}\equiv{\rm std}\left(\log_{10}Q_{\rm vel,local}\right)\,. (26)

We then define, for each cluster,

zvel≡log10⁡Qvel,local−μlog⁡Qσlog⁡Q.z_{\rm vel}\equiv\frac{\log_{10}Q_{\rm vel,local}-\mu_{\log Q}}{\sigma_{\log Q}}\,. (27)

This construction treats the observed ensemble of Qvel,localQ_{\rm vel,local} values across the 79 GCs as an empirical reference distribution. Clusters with zvel≈0z_{\rm vel}\approx 0 have typical levels of local velocity structure for their sample size and other properties; clusters with zvel≫0z_{\rm vel}\gg 0 have unusually structured tangential–velocity distributions, while zvel≪0z_{\rm vel}\ll 0 indicates exceptionally smooth kinematics.

As with the radial component, we adopt

zvel,pos≡max⁡(0,zvel)z_{\rm vel,pos}\equiv\max\left(0,\,z_{\rm vel}\right) (28)

when constructing the crystallization index, so that only positive kinematic anomalies contribute to the final ranking.

III.6 Crystallization index and classification tiers

The radial and kinematic zz–scores, zradz_{\rm rad} and zvelz_{\rm vel}, encode complementary aspects of phase–space structure. We combine them into a single crystallization index by taking their quadrature sum, restricted to their positive parts:

Cindex≡[zrad,pos2+zvel,pos2]1/2.C_{\rm index}\equiv\left[z_{\rm rad,pos}^{2}+z_{\rm vel,pos}^{2}\right]^{1/2}. (29)

By construction, Cindex≈0C_{\rm index}\approx 0 for clusters that are consistent with smooth radial profiles and typical local velocity structure, while Cindex≳2C_{\rm index}\gtrsim 2 corresponds to a cluster that is several standard deviations away from the ensemble in at least one of the two components (or moderately anomalous in both).

In order to organize the subsequent analysis and interpretation, we use CindexC_{\rm index} to define four classification tiers:

  • •

    Tier 1 : clusters with Cindex≥ChighC_{\rm index}\geq C_{\rm high}, where we adopt Chigh=2C_{\rm high}=2 as a fiducial threshold. These systems exhibit the strongest combination of radial and kinematic substructure and are prioritized as crystallization candidates, while still allowing entirely natural dynamical explanations such as rotation, anisotropy, multiple populations, or tidal perturbations.

  • •

    Tier 2 : clusters with Cmid≤Cindex<ChighC_{\rm mid}\leq C_{\rm index}<C_{\rm high}, where Cmid=1C_{\rm mid}=1. These objects show moderate anomalies that may have natural dynamical explanations but are nonetheless interesting.

  • •

    Tier 3 : clusters with 0≤Cindex<Cmid0\leq C_{\rm index}<C_{\rm mid}.

  • •

    Tier 4: clusters with Cindex=0C_{\rm index}=0, these define the control population used to benchmark the behaviour of the metrics and to host the injection experiments described in Section V.

We emphasize that the exact numerical values of the tier thresholds are not critical for the main conclusions; in Section IV we show that the identity of the highest–CC clusters is robust to modest variations in these choices.

III.7 Robustness checks and alternative normalizations

Finally, we test the robustness of the crystallization index against two potential sources of bias: the relative weighting of radial versus kinematic components and the dependence of CindexC_{\rm index} on the total number of stars contributing to each cluster, as well as the choice of radial binning.

First, we explore a family of modified indices of the form

Cf≡[(f​zrad,pos)2+zvel,pos2]1/2,C_{f}\equiv\left[(f\,z_{\rm rad,pos})^{2}+z_{\rm vel,pos}^{2}\right]^{1/2}, (30)

with ff in the range 0.50.5–22. This effectively reweights the importance of radial structure relative to velocity structure. We find that the rank ordering of clusters by CfC_{f} is extremely stable across this range of ff, and that the subset of highest–CC clusters is essentially unchanged; we therefore adopt f=1f=1 as our fiducial choice.

Second, because clusters with more stars naturally permit more precise measurements of structure, we explicitly ”de–trend” the velocity component with sample size. In practice we work with the same Qvel,localQ_{\rm vel,local} values and total star counts N⋆N_{\star} (the number of stars in the working core sample with 0.5≤Rnorm≤3.00.5\leq R_{\rm norm}\leq 3.0) that enter the ensemble calibration of zvelz_{\rm vel} (Section III.5), and fit a simple linear relation of the form

log10⁡Qvel,local=a+b​log10⁡N⋆\log_{10}Q_{\rm vel,local}\;=\;a+b\,\log_{10}N_{\star} (31)

across the full cluster sample. We then form residuals

Δ​log10⁡Q≡log10⁡Qvel,local−(a+b​log10⁡N⋆)\Delta\log_{10}Q\;\equiv\;\log_{10}Q_{\rm vel,local}-\left(a+b\,\log_{10}N_{\star}\right) (32)

and normalize them by their scatter σresid\sigma_{\rm resid} to obtain an N⋆N_{\star}–corrected velocity zz–score,

zvel,resid≡Δ​log10⁡Qσresid,zvel,resid,pos≡max⁡(0,zvel,resid).z_{\rm vel,resid}\;\equiv\;\frac{\Delta\log_{10}Q}{\sigma_{\rm resid}}\,,\qquad z_{\rm vel,resid,pos}\;\equiv\;\max\left(0,\,z_{\rm vel,resid}\right)\,. (33)

The corresponding N⋆N_{\star}–corrected crystallization index used in Section IV.3 is then

Cresid≡[zrad,pos2+zvel,resid,pos2]1/2.C_{\rm resid}\;\equiv\;\left[z_{\rm rad,pos}^{2}+z_{\rm vel,resid,pos}^{2}\right]^{1/2}. (34)

In Section IV.3 we show that this procedure removes most of the monotonic trend of Qvel,localQ_{\rm vel,local} (and hence CindexC_{\rm index}) with N⋆N_{\star}, while the clusters identified as having the highest crystallization remain clear outliers. For the purposes of defining tiers and conducting injection tests we therefore use the uncorrected CindexC_{\rm index} as our primary ranking metric, and treat CresidC_{\rm resid} as a diagnostic for environmental correlations. We additionally use the fixed-NN downsampling experiment described in Section IV.2 as a direct sensitivity check on the impact of sample size, and use log10⁡(tage/trh)\log_{10}(t_{\rm age}/t_{\rm rh}) as a simple dynamical-age proxy when comparing clusters of different relaxation state.

IV Results

IV.1 Global distribution of radial and kinematic structure

We first examine the joint distribution of radial and kinematic structure across the full sample of 79 clusters, as quantified by the standardized metrics zradz_{\rm rad} and zvelz_{\rm vel} defined in Section III. Figure 1 shows zvelz_{\rm vel} versus zradz_{\rm rad} for all clusters, with points color–coded by the crystallization tier defined from CindexC_{\rm index} (Section III.6). The majority of clusters lie near the origin, with |zrad|≲1|z_{\rm rad}|\lesssim 1 and |zvel|≲1|z_{\rm vel}|\lesssim 1, indicating levels of radial and local velocity structure consistent with the null expectations calibrated in Sections III.3 and III.5.

A small set of objects stand out as clear outliers. These high–CC systems populate the upper half of the plane, with zvel≳2z_{\rm vel}\gtrsim 2 and, in some cases, moderately elevated zradz_{\rm rad}. Well–known dynamically complex clusters such as NGC 5139 (Omega Centauri) and NGC 104 (47 Tucanae) fall into this category, as does the system BH 140. The high velocity components for NGC 104 and NGC 5139 are qualitatively consistent with independent proper-motion studies that find non-Gaussian moment structure, including skewness and population-dependent kinematics (Heyl et al., 2017; Ziliotto et al., 2025, 2026). BH 140, by contrast, is primarily radial in our metric (zrad>0z_{\rm rad}>0, zvel<0z_{\rm vel}<0), illustrating that a high CindexC_{\rm index} can arise from spatial inhomogeneity without an anomalous tangential-speed distribution. Conversely, clusters with large zvelz_{\rm vel} but modest zradz_{\rm rad} are candidates for velocity-distribution complexity without a sharp radial overdensity. In our classification scheme these objects are assigned to Tier 1. The one–dimensional distribution of the combined crystallization index CindexC_{\rm index} is shown in Figure 2. The histogram peaks sharply near Cindex≈0C_{\rm index}\approx 0, with a long tail extending to Cindex≳2C_{\rm index}\gtrsim 2. Most clusters occupy the control regime (Tier 4), with Cindex≡0C_{\rm index}\equiv 0, while Tier 3 spans 0<Cindex<10<C_{\rm index}<1 and Tier 2 spans 1<Cindex<21<C_{\rm index}<2. The Tier 1 clusters inhabit the extreme tail. To quantify the statistical significance of this high–CC tail, we compare the observed number of clusters above the Tier 1 threshold to the expectation from an analytic null. Approximating zradz_{\rm rad} and zvelz_{\rm vel} as independent standard normal variables with positive parts contributing to CindexC_{\rm index} (Equation 29), we derive an effective single–cluster probability p0≃0.0566p_{0}\simeq 0.0566 of having Cindex≥Chigh=2C_{\rm index}\geq C_{\rm high}=2. A Monte Carlo experiment drawing 10510^{5} ensembles of 79 clusters from the analytic null yields a fraction ≃8.3×10−1\simeq 8.3\times 10^{-1} of realizations with Nhigh≥3N_{\rm high}\geq 3, indicating that the observed high–CC tail is fully consistent with the smooth baseline and does not constitute a statistically significant excess. In what follows we therefore treat the Tier 1 systems as the high end of the crystallization distribution, without claiming a global departure from the analytic null model.

IV.4 Tier populations and control sample

The distributions discussed above naturally partition the cluster population into the four tiers defined in Section III.6. Tier 1 consists of three clusters in the high–CindexC_{\rm index} tail (Cindex≳2C_{\rm index}\gtrsim 2). These clusters are prime targets for more detailed dynamical modelling because they are metric outliers; the tier label should not be read as evidence for non-standard physics or engineering. Known mechanisms such as multiple populations, rotation, anisotropy, tides, accretion history, or catalogue systematics remain the default explanations to test first.

Tier 2 and 3 comprises 52 clusters with intermediate and low crystallization indices (0<Cindex<20<C_{\rm index}<2). Many of these show modest enhancements in either zradz_{\rm rad} or zvelz_{\rm vel} individually, but do not stand out as strongly as the Tier 1 systems in the joint (zrad,zvel)(z_{\rm rad},z_{\rm vel}) plane. While their structure is likely explicable in terms of known dynamical processes (e.g. internal rotation, recent tidal perturbations, or residual substructure from formation), they remain useful comparison objects.

Tier 4 collects 24 clusters with Cindex=0C_{\rm index}=0, including a subset of 10 (control group). The latter systems have both zrad≤0z_{\rm rad}\leq 0 and zvel≤0z_{\rm vel}\leq 0 and behave, within uncertainties, like smooth null realizations of their radial and local velocity distributions. These make up our control pool for the injection tests described in Section V. By adding artificial ultra–cold components of varying strength to Tier 4 clusters and re–evaluating CindexC_{\rm index}, we are able to quantify the sensitivity of our method and to translate the non–detection of high crystallization in these controls into upper limits on any ultra–cold kinematic substructure.

V Injection Tests and Detection Limits

The crystallization index CindexC_{\rm index} provides a relative ranking of clusters by their phase–space structure, but by itself it does not directly answer the question: how sensitive is our method to a genuinely cold, coherent component, if such a component were present? In this section we address that question by performing controlled injection experiments in dynamically smooth Tier 4 clusters, and by translating the absence of high crystallization in these controls into quantitative upper limits on ultra–cold kinematic substructures.

V.1 Signal model and experimental setup

We model a hypothetical crystallized component as a population of stars confined to a limited radial range and occupying an extremely narrow region of tangential velocity space. Motivated by the construction of Qvel,localQ_{\rm vel,local} in Section III.4, we work in units of the shell–level dispersion σs\sigma_{s} and define

x≡vtanσs,x\equiv\frac{v_{\rm tan}}{\sigma_{s}}\,, (35)

where vtanv_{\rm tan} is the tangential speed in a given radial shell and σs\sigma_{s} is the robust IQR–based dispersion of the original shell velocities (before any injection). Under the null hypothesis of an isotropic, Gaussian velocity field, the distribution of xx is approximately Rayleigh with unit scale. A cold, crystallized component is therefore characterized by a strongly peaked excess at x≪1x\ll 1.

In our injection experiments we consider an idealized signal of the form

pcrystal​(x)∝exp⁡[−(x−x0)22​σx2],p_{\rm crystal}(x)\propto\exp\left[-\frac{(x-x_{0})^{2}}{2\sigma_{x}^{2}}\right]\,, (36)

with x0≈0.1x_{0}\approx 0.1 and σx≈0.01\sigma_{x}\approx 0.01, i.e. a very narrow spike in normalized tangential speed close to zero. We confine this component to the intermediate radial shell,

shell2:1.0≤Rnorm<2.0,{\rm shell~2}:\quad 1.0\leq R_{\rm norm}<2.0\,, (37)

because this shell is the best-populated radial region in the adopted samples and avoids the most severe central crowding and outer-field contamination. It is therefore the most favorable single-shell location for a first sensitivity experiment, not a claim that real substructures must preferentially form there. Within shell 2 we select a fraction ff of the stars and replace their original vtanv_{\rm tan} values by draws from pcrystal​(x)p_{\rm crystal}(x), converted back to physical units using the shell’s original σs\sigma_{s}. The remaining stars in the shell, and all stars in shells 1 and 3, are left unchanged.

For each choice of ff we recompute Qvel,localQ_{\rm vel,local}, log10⁡Qvel,local\log_{10}Q_{\rm vel,local}, zvelz_{\rm vel}, and the resulting crystallization index. We perform this procedure for a grid of signal strengths ff between 0 and 1. In practice we adopt a small discrete set of values (e.g. f=0, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.40, 0.60, 0.80, 1.0f=0,\,0.05,\,0.10,\,0.20,\,0.40,\,0.60,\,0.80,\,1.0), since the dependence of the metrics on ff is smooth on these scales.

  • •

    Global ensemble: We use the full set of 79 clusters to define the mean and dispersion of log10⁡Qvel,local\log_{10}Q_{\rm vel,local},

    zvel,all=log10⁡Qvel,local−μlog⁡Q,allσlog⁡Q,allz_{\rm vel,all}=\frac{\log_{10}Q_{\rm vel,local}-\mu_{\log Q,{\rm all}}}{\sigma_{\log Q,{\rm all}}} (39)

    Call=[zrad,pos2+zvel,all,pos2]1/2,C_{\rm all}=\left[z_{\rm rad,pos}^{2}+z_{\rm vel,all,pos}^{2}\right]^{1/2}, (40)

  • •

    zvel,ctrl=log10⁡Qvel,local−μlog⁡Q,ctrlσlog⁡Q,ctrl.z_{\rm vel,ctrl}=\frac{\log_{10}Q_{\rm vel,local}-\mu_{\log Q,{\rm ctrl}}}{\sigma_{\log Q,{\rm ctrl}}}. (42)

    Cctrl=[zrad,pos2+zvel,ctrl,pos2]1/2C_{\rm ctrl}=\left[z_{\rm rad,pos}^{2}+z_{\rm vel,ctrl,pos}^{2}\right]^{1/2} (43)

In both cases we hold the radial structure metric zradz_{\rm rad} fixed, since the injection modifies only the tangential velocities in shell 2. For Tier 4 controls, zrad,posz_{\rm rad,pos} is typically zero or small.

V.4 Response of the crystallization index to injected crystals

Figure 7 shows, for each of the six host clusters, how the crystallization index responds to an injected ultra–cold component as a function of the injected fraction ff of shell–2 stars. In every panel the solid circles trace the ”global” index CallC_{\rm all}, where zvelz_{\rm vel} is calibrated using the full 79–cluster ensemble, while the dashed squares show the ”control” index CctrlC_{\rm ctrl}, where zvelz_{\rm vel} is calibrated only against the smooth, Tier 4 population.

At small and intermediate ff the behaviour is qualitatively similar in all hosts. As more stars in shell 2 are reassigned to the ultra–cold component, the local tangential–velocity histogram becomes increasingly bimodal, with a narrow peak near vtan≃0v_{\rm tan}\simeq 0 superimposed on a broader ”thermal” background. This drives Qvel,localQ_{\rm vel,local} above its baseline value and causes both CallC_{\rm all} and CctrlC_{\rm ctrl} to rise. At fixed injected fraction, richer clusters are more sensitive rather than less sensitive because the number of stars in the anomalous velocity bin scales with NsN_{s}, while the Poisson uncertainty scales only as Ns\sqrt{N_{s}}; the chi-square contributions in Equation 24 therefore grow as the feature becomes better resolved. The rate at which CC increases with ff depends strongly on NcoreN_{\rm core}: in the very well–sampled cluster NGC 6121 (Ncore≃1.6×104N_{\rm core}\simeq 1.6\times 10^{4}), CctrlC_{\rm ctrl} crosses the Tier 1 threshold Chigh=2C_{\rm high}=2 already by f∼0.16f\sim 0.16, whereas in lower–NN systems such as IC 1276, a much larger crystal fraction is required to produce a comparable increase.

A striking feature of Figure 7 is that, in several hosts, CC does not grow indefinitely with ff: once ff approaches unity, both CallC_{\rm all} and especially CctrlC_{\rm ctrl} drop back towards values of order unity, and in some cases nearly to zero. This does not mean that a nearly pure crystal would be dynamically unstructured. Instead, it is a consequence of how Qvel,localQ_{\rm vel,local} is defined. In each radial shell we first estimate a robust dispersion σs\sigma_{s} from the current velocities and then work in normalized units x=vtan/σsx=v_{\rm tan}/\sigma_{s}, comparing the resulting histogram to a unit–scale Rayleigh distribution. When only a modest fraction of stars belong to the ultra–cold component, σs\sigma_{s} is still set mainly by the hot background and the cold spike appears as a strong deviation from the Rayleigh form, maximizing Qvel,localQ_{\rm vel,local} and therefore CC. The apparent peak near f≃0.7f\simeq 0.7–0.8 in several panels is the transition where the IQR-based scale estimate begins to be controlled by the injected cold component rather than by the original hot background. When ff is pushed close to 1, the cold component itself determines σs\sigma_{s}, the entire distribution becomes very narrow, and after rescaling by this smaller σs\sigma_{s} the normalized velocities again resemble a single Rayleigh–like distribution. In other words, our statistic is most sensitive to mixtures of hot and cold populations; once the crystal completely dominates the shell, the velocity field becomes effectively single–component and Qvel,localQ_{\rm vel,local} decreases.

This behaviour has two important implications. First, for the range of ff that we consider relevant for realistic substructures (f≲0.6f\lesssim 0.6 in shell 2), the metric behaves monotonically in the sense that larger cold fractions produce larger CC, and hence the injected crystals are clearly detected whenever they involve a sufficiently large fraction of the shell–2 stars. Second, the drop in CC at very high ff reminds us that our present metric is not optimized for the extreme scenario of an entire shell (or entire cluster) being rearranged into a single ultra–cold component. Our injection tests and the resulting limits should therefore be interpreted as constraints on embedded cold components in an otherwise ”normal” background, not as a fully general search for arbitrarily exotic phase–space configurations.

V.5 Detection thresholds and upper limits

The injection experiments described above can be summarized in terms of approximate detection thresholds on the fraction of stars that can participate in an ultra–cold component without driving the cluster into the high–CC regime. For each host cluster we define a 90% detection threshold fshell,90f_{{\rm shell},90} as the smallest value of ff for which the control–ensemble crystallization index CctrlC_{\rm ctrl} exceeds the Tier 1 threshold Chigh=2C_{\rm high}=2 in at least 90%90\% of the Monte Carlo realizations. Table 1 lists fshell,90f_{{\rm shell},90} for each host, together with the total number of core stars NcoreN_{\rm core} in the range 0.5≤Rnorm≤3.00.5\leq R_{\rm norm}\leq 3.0.

Taken together, these results imply that in the best–sampled control clusters, NGC 6121 and NGC 4833, our crystallization metric would be sensitive to ultra–cold, shell–confined components involving only ≃0.2\simeq 0.2 of the shell–2 stars (fshell,90≈0.16f_{{\rm shell},90}\approx 0.16–0.19). Since shell 2 typically contains roughly half of the stars in the 0.5≤Rnorm≤3.00.5\leq R_{\rm norm}\leq 3.0 core sample for these systems, this corresponds to detecting cold structures that comprise only a few to several percent of all core stars. Intermediate–NN hosts such as NGC 6093 and NGC 6838 require somewhat larger injected fractions (fshell,90≈0.3f_{{\rm shell},90}\approx 0.3–0.36), while in the low–NN host IC 1276 we achieve 90% detection probability only when the crystal dominates shell 2 (fshell,90≃0.6f_{{\rm shell},90}\simeq 0.6). In the extreme low–NN case of Ter 3, even an injection with f=1f=1 fails to produce Pdet,ctrl≥0.9P_{\rm det,ctrl}\geq 0.9, underscoring the limited constraining power of very sparsely sampled clusters.

Because these six Tier 4 hosts span NcoreN_{\rm core} from ∼8×102\sim 8\times 10^{2} to nearly 1.6×1041.6\times 10^{4} and cover a range of structural properties, they provide a reasonable indication of the sensitivity of our method across the broader control population. In the absence of high crystallization signals in the Tier 4 sample, we can therefore state that no ultra–cold, single–shell tangential–velocity components of the type modelled here, involving more than at most a few to ∼10\sim 10–20% of the core stars (depending on NcoreN_{\rm core}), are present in the Gaia–accessible core regions of these dynamically smooth globular clusters.

VI Discussion

VI.1 Interpretation of the crystallization index

Our crystallization framework is deliberately model–light: rather than fitting detailed dynamical models to each cluster, we use simple, standardized statistics to quantify how far a system deviates from a smooth, quasi–equilibrium baseline. The two ingredients, QradQ_{\rm rad} and Qvel,localQ_{\rm vel,local} (Section III), probe complementary aspects of projected phase–space structure.

The radial metric QradQ_{\rm rad} asks whether the normalized projected radius distribution RnormR_{\rm norm} in the range 0.5≤Rnorm≤30.5\leq R_{\rm norm}\leq 3 can be described, up to noise, by a smooth one–dimensional profile. By construction, QradQ_{\rm rad} is insensitive to the overall scale of the profile (which is removed by using Rnorm=Rarcmin/rh,arcminR_{\rm norm}=R_{\rm arcmin}/r_{h,{\rm arcmin}}) and to Poisson fluctuations at very low counts (mitigated by the moving–average smoothing in Equation 13). Large QradQ_{\rm rad} values correspond to significant excesses or deficits in one or more radial bins relative to a smoothed reference profile ”learned” from the cluster’s own data. Physically, this can arise from tidal distortions, shells, clumps, or strong departures from a single–scale profile.

The kinematic metric Qvel,localQ_{\rm vel,local} asks a different question: in a given radial shell, is the one–dimensional tangential–velocity distribution consistent with the Rayleigh law expected for the norm of a two–dimensional Gaussian velocity field? Here the normalization by a shell–level robust dispersion σs\sigma_{s} removes the overall scale of the velocity field, so that only the shape of the distribution matters. Large Qvel,localQ_{\rm vel,local} values mean that, after this normalization, the histogram of x=vtan/σsx=v_{\rm tan}/\sigma_{s} deviates strongly from the Rayleigh reference in a chi–square sense (Section III.4), typically because of pronounced wings, multi–modality, or an overabundance of very slow or very fast stars.

By converting both raw metrics to standardized zz–scores and combining them into

Cindex≡zrad,pos2+zvel,pos2,C_{\rm index}\equiv\sqrt{z_{\rm rad,pos}^{2}+z_{\rm vel,pos}^{2}}\,, (44)

we effectively embed each cluster in a two–dimensional space of ”how radially lumpy” and ”how kinematically odd” it is compared to the population as a whole. The fact that known dynamically complex systems such as ω\omega Cen and 47 Tuc rank highly in CindexC_{\rm index}, while many ordinary clusters lie near Cindex≈0C_{\rm index}\approx 0, suggests that the metric is successfully capturing real physical diversity in dynamical states, without being tied to any specific microphysical scenario.

VI.2 Relation to classical views of globular cluster structure

There is a large literature on the structural and dynamical complexity of Galactic globular clusters, including multiple stellar populations, internal rotation, anisotropy, higher-order proper-motion moments, and tidal features (e.g. Gratton et al., 2012; Milone and Marino, 2022; Kamann et al., 2018; Heyl et al., 2017; Ziliotto et al., 2025, 2026). In that context, our crystallization index should be viewed as a minimalist ensemble-screening statistic, not a replacement for population-resolved kinematic modelling. Moment-based diagnostics such as skewness and kurtosis measure the shape of the proper-motion component distributions directly, often as functions of stellar population and radius. Our Qvel,localQ_{\rm vel,local} instead compresses the radial-shell distribution of cluster-centric speed magnitudes into a Rayleigh-deviation score, while QradQ_{\rm rad} adds a separate spatial term.

The connection is nevertheless physically meaningful. NGC 104 and NGC 5139, two of our high-CindexC_{\rm index} clusters, are also systems where independent studies find strong non-Gaussian or population-dependent proper-motion structure. This agreement suggests that zvelz_{\rm vel} is sensitive to at least some of the same higher-order kinematic complexity captured by skewness/kurtosis analyses, even though it does not preserve the full vector information. Conversely, BH 140 illustrates the complementarity of the radial channel: it is high-CC because of zradz_{\rm rad} rather than zvelz_{\rm vel}, and would not be highlighted by a velocity-moment diagnostic alone.

Physically, high CindexC_{\rm index} can be produced by several standard mechanisms. Multiple stellar populations can have different radial concentrations and velocity anisotropies; rotation and perspective effects can distort the local speed distribution; tidal stripping, disk or bulge shocks, and eccentric orbits can create radial asymmetries or cold debris; incomplete phase mixing after early mergers or accretion can preserve coherent substructure; and mass segregation, binaries, or retained remnants can reshape local velocity distributions. Catalogue effects, especially crowding-dependent incompleteness or residual field contamination, also remain possible for individual clusters. The metric is therefore best used as a triage tool for deciding where more detailed chemodynamical modelling is warranted.

The correlation analysis in Section IV.3 supports this interpretation. Raw CindexC_{\rm index} depends strongly on NcoreN_{\rm core}, weakly on mass, and modestly on a relaxation-time proxy, while CresidC_{\rm resid} reduces the mass and sample-size trends but retains substantial scatter. Crystallization therefore does not reduce to a simple function of one global parameter.

Our approach is complementary to more detailed dynamical modelling, such as Jeans or NN–body fits to rotation fields, anisotropy profiles, higher–order moments, and multi-epoch astrometric catalogues (e.g. Kamann et al., 2018; Libralato et al., 2022). Those methods attempt to reconstruct a physically motivated phase–space distribution function for each cluster individually. By contrast, CindexC_{\rm index} treats each cluster as a point in a low–dimensional summary space, making it easier to perform ensemble–level comparisons and to identify outliers that deserve more detailed follow–up.

VI.3 Implications for globular clusters as technosignature targets

Several authors have argued that globular clusters may be promising environments for long–lived technological civilizations, owing to their old ages, long dynamical lifetimes, and small typical separations between stars (Di Stefano and Ray, 2016). In parallel, this prospect has recently motivated an increasing number of dedicated technosignature surveys targeting globular clusters(Huang et al., 2025). In such a scenario, the phase–space structure of the cluster becomes directly relevant to questions of habitability and interstellar engineering: the local velocity dispersion, tidal field, and long–term stability of orbits all influence the ease of travel, infrastructure building, and communication within the cluster.

The low metallicities of many GCs are an important caveat for any habitability or technosignature interpretation. Metal-poor environments may suppress the formation of giant planets, and early searches for short-period planets in 47 Tuc found no detections at the surveyed sensitivity (Gilliland et al., 2000). On the other hand, the dependence of small-planet occurrence on metallicity is weaker than that of giant planets (Buchhave et al., 2012), and the dense, old nature of GCs motivates technosignature searches for reasons that are not identical to ordinary exoplanet yield optimization. We therefore present CindexC_{\rm index} only as a dynamical target-prioritization layer, not as a claim that high- or low-CC clusters are intrinsically more habitable.

Our crystallization index does not attempt to model technosignatures explicitly, but it does provide a quantitative handle on how dynamically ”ordinary” or ”exceptional” a given cluster is, relative to the Milky Way GC population. One can imagine at least two broad, complementary ways to use this information in a SETI context:

  • •

    Conservative strategy: target smooth, dynamically ordinary clusters. If one hypothesizes that long–lived, distributed technological activity is most likely to arise in clusters that are dynamically stable and free from extreme tidal or merger events, then low–CindexC_{\rm index} clusters become attractive targets: they are old, relaxed systems whose present–day phase–space structure closely matches simple equilibrium expectations. In this view, Tier 4 clusters define a baseline for ”quiet” environments in which technosignatures, if present, might be driven more by internal social and technological processes than by external dynamical perturbations.

  • •

    Exploratory strategy: target dynamically exceptional clusters. Alternatively, one may speculate that clusters with unusual dynamical histories, those with large CindexC_{\rm index}, could be special in ways that are also relevant for the emergence or survival of technology. For example, extreme retention of remnants, merger histories, or strong internal rotation might correlate with atypical planet formation environments, radiation fields, or long–term stability of habitable zones. In this speculative picture, Tier 1 clusters would be natural laboratories to search for unconventional technosignatures or large–scale engineering projects.

VI.4 Limitations and avenues for improvement

Several limitations of the current study are worth highlighting. First, our analysis uses Gaia-based proper motions and parallaxes to construct plane-of-sky, cluster-centric tangential velocities; line-of-sight velocities are only implicit in the membership and cluster-parameter catalogues. A full 3D treatment, combining Gaia with large spectroscopic surveys and integral-field spectroscopy (e.g. Kamann et al., 2018), would allow more powerful anisotropy and phase-space diagnostics. Second, related astrometric resources such as the HST Astrometry Catalog of Galactic Globular Clusters (Libralato et al., 2022) and population-resolved proper-motion studies (e.g. Ziliotto et al., 2025, 2026) already explore several of these directions for selected clusters; our contribution is the homogeneous ensemble-level scalar rather than the definitive kinematic model of any one cluster. Third, we normalize radii by the projected half-light radius rhr_{h}, which is convenient but may blur information about multi-scale structures such as distinct cores, envelopes, and extended tidal features. Alternative normalizations or multi-scale versions of QradQ_{\rm rad} could be explored. Fourth, the null models for QradQ_{\rm rad} and Qvel,localQ_{\rm vel,local} are intentionally simple. Real clusters may deviate from these baselines in ways that are entirely consistent with standard dynamics. Finally, our injected “crystals” are extremely narrow in normalized velocity and confined to a single shell. The limits in Section V therefore apply only to embedded ultra-cold single-shell components of the specified form, not to all possible exotic or natural substructures.

Despite these limitations, the present crystallization analysis demonstrates that simple, ensemble–normalized metrics can reveal meaningful structure in the GC population and can be pushed to the point of providing quantitative non–detections of certain classes of cold substructure. This suggests several natural extensions: coupling CindexC_{\rm index} to multi–population maps (e.g. Gratton et al., 2012; Milone and Marino, 2022), incorporating 3D velocities and orbits, and embedding the framework in forward models that connect hypothetical engineered phase–space structures to observable kinematic signatures.

VII Conclusions

We have introduced a simple, Gaia–anchored framework for quantifying “kinematic crystallization” in Galactic globular clusters. Starting from the homogeneous catalogues of Baumgardt and Vasiliev (2021); Vasiliev and Baumgardt (2021a), we constructed a working sample of 79 clusters with well–measured projected radii and tangential velocities (Section II). Two model–light statistics summarize their small–scale phase–space structure: a radial inhomogeneity metric QradQ_{\rm rad} that measures bin–to–bin lumpiness in the normalized radius distribution RnormR_{\rm norm}, and a local tangential–velocity metric Qvel,localQ_{\rm vel,local} that tests for deviations from the Rayleigh form expected for an isotropic 2D Gaussian field (Section III). After standardizing these to zz–scores and combining their positive parts in quadrature, we obtain a crystallization index CindexC_{\rm index} that ranks clusters by the joint extremeness of their radial and kinematic structure.

Across the 79–cluster ensemble, the distribution of (zrad,zvel)(z_{\rm rad},z_{\rm vel}) is strongly peaked near (0,0)(0,0), with most systems having Cindex<1C_{\rm index}<1 and therefore lying in our Tier 2–4 regime. Only three clusters exceed the Tier 1 threshold Chigh=2C_{\rm high}=2, and even these lie within the broad expectations of an analytic smooth–null model: approximating zradz_{\rm rad} and zvelz_{\rm vel} as independent standard normal variates, one expects 4.5±2.04.5\pm 2.0 high–CC clusters out of 79, and the probability of observing at least three is ≃0.83\simeq 0.83 (Section IV.1). Thus the high–CC tail is not itself a statistically significant excess over a smooth baseline, but the Tier 1 clusters remain individually interesting as dynamically complex outliers. A four–tier classification based on CindexC_{\rm index} (Tier 1: C≥2C\geq 2; Tier 2: 1≤C<21\leq C<2; Tier 3: 0<C<10<C<1; Tier 4: C=0C=0) provides a convenient vocabulary for describing this diversity.

We have shown that CindexC_{\rm index} is partly sensitive to sample size and must be interpreted accordingly. The raw index correlates with log10⁡Ncore\log_{10}N_{\rm core} (ρ=0.57\rho=0.57), while the N⋆N_{\star}–corrected index CresidC_{\rm resid} removes this trend to first order (Section IV.2). Correlations with present-day mass are weak, and a dynamical-age proxy shows the expected tendency for more relaxed clusters to have lower crystallization (Section IV.3). We do not present a metallicity correlation in this work because metallicity is not part of the homogeneous input table used by the analysis.

To quantify what kinds of ultra–cold components our metric would detect, we performed controlled injection experiments in six low–CC clusters drawn from the Tier 4 pool: IC 1276, NGC 4833, NGC 6093, NGC 6121, NGC 6838, and Ter 3 (Section V). Into each cluster we injected synthetic, extremely cold tangential–velocity components confined to 1≤Rnorm<21\leq R_{\rm norm}<2 and occupying a fraction ff of shell–2 stars. Using the Tier 4 population to calibrate the velocity zz–scores, we measured the detection probability that the control–ensemble index CctrlC_{\rm ctrl} exceeds the Tier 1 threshold. The resulting 90% detection limits span fshell,90≃0.16f_{{\rm shell},90}\simeq 0.16–0.19 in the best–sampled hosts (NGC 6121 and NGC 4833), fshell,90≃0.3f_{{\rm shell},90}\simeq 0.3–0.36 in intermediate–NN systems (NGC 6093, NGC 6838), and ≳0.6\gtrsim 0.6 or undetected over our grid in the lowest–NN controls (IC 1276, Ter 3). In the well–sampled clusters this corresponds to cold components involving only a few percent of core stars, whereas in sparse systems only nearly dominant crystals would be detectable. The absence of strong crystallization signals in the Tier 3–4 sample therefore rules out ultra–cold, single–shell structures of this type at the level of ∼\simfew–to–∼\sim20% of the core stars, within the 0.5≤Rnorm≤3.00.5\leq R_{\rm norm}\leq 3.0 region accessible to Gaia.

Taken together, these results suggest that the present–day Milky Way globular cluster system is largely consistent with smooth, equilibrium phase–space structure at the resolution probed here, punctuated by a small number of dynamically complex outliers but lacking any “smoking gun” crystallization that would demand non–standard physics or artificial engineering. The crystallization index provides a compact way to rank clusters by dynamical extremeness, to select targets for deeper chemodynamical modelling, and to define a controlled, low–CC baseline for future injection and sensitivity studies. Extensions that incorporate full 3D velocities, explicit multiple–population information, and forward modelling of specific crystallization scenarios—natural or artificial—will further sharpen the connection between observed phase–space structure, cluster formation and evolution, and any putative technosignatures in dense stellar environments.
