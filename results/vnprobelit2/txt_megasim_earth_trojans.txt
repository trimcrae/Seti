Abstract
Using N-body simulation results from the MEGASIM dataset , we present spatial distributions of Earth Trojan Asteroids and assess the detectability of the population in current and next-generation ground-based astronomical surveys (Yeager & Golovich, 2022a ) . Our high-fidelity Earth Trojan Asteroid (ETA) distribution maps show never-before-seen high-resolution spatial features that evolve over timescales up to 1 Gyr. The simulation was synchronized to start times and timelines of two observational astronomy surveys, 1) the Vera C. Rubin Observatory’s Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) and 2) the Zwicky Transient Facility (ZTF). We calculate u pper limits for the number of ETAs potentially observable with both the ZTF and LSST surveys . Due to the Yarkovsky Effect, we find no stable ETAs on billion year timescales likely to be detected by any ETA survey, as no C-type or S-type ETAs (with H < 22 𝐻 22 H<22 and H < 24 𝐻 24 H<24 , respectively) are likely to be stable on billion year timescales , and ETAs large enough to remain stable on billion year timescales are very rare relative to the rest of the ETA population. We find that a twilight ETA survey will not drastically increase the likelihood of individual ETA detection, but would provide orders of magnitude more observations of select ETA populations. The null detection to date from ZTF restricts the potential ETA population to hundreds of objects larger than 100 meters (at H ≈ 22 𝐻 22 H\approx 22 , Lifset et al., 2021 ) , while a null detection by LSST will further restrict th e ETA population to tens of objects larger than 100 meters .

1 Introduction

Trojan asteroids 1 1 1 The term ‘Trojan’ generally refers to objects co-orbiting the Sun with Jupiter; however, given the general nature of these minor bodies across the Solar System, we use the term ‘Trojan’ generically to refer to which planet they co-orbit the Sun with . are a class of co-orbiting objects that librate about the L4 or L5 Lagrangian points within a planet’s orbit. Mapping the distributions of possible Earth Trojan Asteroids (ETAs) is important to guide observational searches , as ETAs may answer pressing science questions about how our Solar System and Moon were formed . Librations of ETAs can carry an asteroid nearly the entire distance between the planet they are co-orbiting with, and its L3 Lagrange point on the far side of the orbit behind the Sun. In a rotating reference frame, the gravitational potential lines look like tadpoles , where these tadpole orbits occupy a half-toroid al region, which includes either the L4 or L5 point within . The region of the sky where ETAs potentially exist is difficult to observe due to the low Solar elongation. The geometry also tends to have small reflection angles, making observations even more difficult from Earth. However, synoptic surveys such as those carried out by the Zwicky Transient Facility (ZTF, Bellm et al., 2018 ) and the Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST Science Collaboration et al., 2009 ; Jones et al., 2020 ) could still overcome the difficult observing conditions using numerous observations at the periphery of the ETA regime. Twilight surveys, in particular, could be powerful probes into the population of ETAs of interest; ZTF has been carrying out a twilight survey for years (see e.g., Ye et al., 2020 ) , and there is current discussion on whether or not the Rubin Observatory’s Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) will or will not have a dedicated twilight survey (Seaman et al., 2018 ) .

1.1 Scientific Impact of ETAs

There are several potential sourcing mechanisms for Trojan asteroids : 1) they may be formed in the early proto-planetary disk near a planet’s L4 or L5 Lagrange point (Montesinos et al., 2020 ) , 2) they may be captured debris produced from major planetary impacts , and 3) they may be sourced from perturbed populations of asteroids elsewhere in the Solar System ( either during planetary migration or from ejected asteroids from the main-asteroid belt ) . The first two mechanisms would have occurred early in the formation of the Solar System, and we consider these to be “primordial” in nature. For ETAs, primordial sourcing through the impact hypothesis may also explain lunar formation. The giant impact hypothesis has been proposed, suggesting that a Mars-sized proto-planet could have formed in a stable orbit among debris , at either Earth’s L4 or L5 Lagrange point , and collided with proto-Earth from a 1 AU orbit in the solar nebula to form the M oon (Belbruno & Gott, 2005 ) . It is hypothesized that a debris cloud from such a collision could have provided a primordial source of ETAs (Canup, 2004 ) . If found, such a population of asteroids would offer a unique lens into the evolution of the early Solar System and the Earth - Moon system. The existence of primordial ETAs would offer ideal low - Δ ​ v Δ 𝑣 \Delta v targets for rendezvous missions to study the early inner S olar S ystem , especially the Earth-Moon system (Malhotra, 2019 ) . NASA ’s Lucy mission will soon begin its 12-year journey to several families of Jupiter Trojans, which will begin to answer many of the scientific questions we have discussed (Zhou et al., 2020 ) . While the Lucy mission is focused on primordial Jupiter Trojans, it may shed light on how scientifically rich these objects are, validating that future Earth Trojan missions will be important in understanding our Solar System.

1.3 Previous ETA S imulations

We refer readers to our previous work in analyzing the MEGASIM results (described in more detail in § 2 ) for an in-depth analysis on lifetime and stability of ETAs, including a detailed literature review (Yeager & Golovich, 2022a ) . Numerous additional ETA detectability studies have been carried out using numerical simulations , to understand and constrain ETA populations for guiding observers ( e.g., Wiegert et al., 2000 ; Evans & Tabachnik, 2000 ; Morais & Morbidelli, 2002 ; Todd et al., 2012 ; Zhou et al., 2019 ; Lifset et al., 2021 ; Napier et al., 2022 ) , and have typically centered around the questions of sourcing, lifetimes, stability zones, and observability of the stable population. Wiegert et al. ( 2000 ) produced spatial maps of time-averaged ETA positions from simulations of two synthetic populations of Earth Trojans by injecting co-orbiting Earth asteroids uniformly within the region between the L2 and L3 points. When viewing these ETA orbits from Earth, the resulting spatial density peak is a few degrees displaced toward the Sun from the L4 and L5 Lagrange points. Evans & Tabachnik ( 2000 ) and Morais & Morbidelli ( 2002 ) found stable zones with inclination between 10° and 45° , with a wide range of ecliptic longitudes encapsulating the L4 and L5 points , but spanning tens of degrees to either side of L4 or L5. Todd et al. ( 2012 ) modeled albedo, size, and orbital distributions of ETAs in an effort to define an ‘optimal’ search strategy for ETA detection , and based on their simulations, suggest a twilight observing campaign to search the wide swath of area that the ETAs in their simulation cover. The only known asteroid at the time of these simulations , 2010 ​ T ​ K 7 2010 𝑇 subscript 𝐾 7 2010TK_{7} , would have appeared in such a survey (Todd et al., 2012 ) . While currently known ETAs exhibit orbital stability on the order of thousands of years, objects librating about the Earth’s L4 or L5 Lagrange points can be stable on the order of millions or billions of years (Yeager & Golovich, 2022a ) . An ETA’s ability to remain stable depends on several factors, e.g., spin rate, shape, size, albedo, density, and surface conductivity , of which play a role in non-gravitational forces such as the Yarkovsky Effect. Smaller ETAs experience larger effects from Solar radiation, thus driving them from the co-orbital regime more quickly. Simulations by (Zhou et al., 2019 ) find that only ETAs larger than ∼ similar-to \sim 100 meters remain stable for 1 Gyr. Lifset et al. ( 2021 ) placed upper limit constraints on the number of ETAs in L4, as described in § 1.2 .

In this paper, we study the MEGASIM data to assess the observability of ETAs in ongoing and upcoming synoptic surveys. In § 2 , we give a brief summary of the simulation details, and we describe our method of determining the flux of individual asteroids in the simulation. In § 3 , we present spatial distributions and discuss translating our simulation output into observational surveys. In § 4 , we present our results regarding the observability of ETAs in two synoptic surveys. Finally, in § 5 , we offer conclusions of our analysis.

2 MEGASIM

The Multitudinous Earth Greek Asteroid Simulation (MEGASIM), announced first in Yeager & Golovich ( 2022b ) , resulted in 11.2 million ETA orbits initialized on trajectories that span beyond the expected ETA stability regime around the L4 Lagrange point. The orbits have now been carried out to a maximum of 4.5 billion years, allowing a study of the longevity of ETA orbits.

2.1 Initialization and Parallelization

The MEGASIM data set consists of two distinct simulations , one using the WHFast integrator (Wisdom & Holman, 1991 ; Rein & Tamayo, 2015 ) , and the other using the IAS15 (Rein & Spiegel, 2015 ) integrator (see Yeager & Golovich ( 2022a ) for additional detail on these data sets) . This work focuses on the WHFast data set , as the WHFast integrator is significantly faster than other symplectic integrators, enabling us to integrate to the full 4.5 billion year age of the Solar System. To achieve parallelization, the 11.2 million ETA trajectories were placed into batches of 500, resulting in 22,400 parallel simulated S olar S ystems that were integrated forward in time . Each simulated S olar System is initialized with the Sun and eight planets (i.e., Mercury, Venus, Earth-Moon, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune ), with initial planetary positions gathered from the JPL HORIZONS system 3 3 3 https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/horizons/ at an epoch of JD2459145.61625984 (Giorgini et al., 1996 ) . We utilized the Catalyst, Quartz, and Ruby clusters managed by Livermore Computing at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory 4 4 4 https://hpc.llnl.gov/hardware/platforms/ruby .

Each batch of asteroids were initialized with six orbital parameters selected from a random distribution, as described below. Each model was then integrated forward with REBOUND 5 5 5 https://github.com/hannorein/rebound ( Rein & Liu , 2012 ) . The integration time step was set to one-day, and position and velocity information for each simulation was output every 1000 years. Each ETA orbit was initialized with a true longitude ( θ 𝜃 \theta ) randomly and uniformly distributed between Earth and L3 (on the side of Earth’s orbit containing L4). The argument of pericenter and longitude of ascending node w ere randomly assigned values between 0 and 2 ​ π 2 𝜋 2\pi , assuming a uniform distribution. 2.3 The LSST and ZTF Surveys

We use the LSST v2.2 baseline and twilight OpSim runs 6 6 6 https://www.lsst.org/scientists/simulations/opsim (‘lsst_no_twilight_neo_v2.2’ and ‘twi_neo_repeat4_riz_v2.2_10yrs’, respectively), as well as all ZTF observations spanning dates 2018-03-17 to 2022-06-01 (LSST project, 2022 ; Bellm et al., 2018 ) , as the survey data for our analysis (see section 4 ). The number of observations in the LSST baseline OpSim was 2,259,570 and twilight 2,078,065. We filter down the ZTF data set to only include 30-second exposures taken by the Palomar P48 observing system, and we use both public survey data and private institutional partnership observational data. The resulting dataset consists of 706,459 30-second observations.

2.4 Estimating Survey Losses

To be considered a detection, one of two conditions must be satisfied: 1) three pairs of observations (at minimum) are required within a month-long rolling window, or 2) two observations are required within one hour of one another. These conditions are reasonable for orbit fitting between observations (e.g., Jedicke, 2006 ; Denneau et al., 2009 ) and are to be used by LSST (Jones et al., 2018 ) . Survey detections can be lost from non-ideal conditions such as observation linking, trailing losses from streaking across a detector (i.e., the CCD), and chip gaps between pixels on the detector. Loss of detection due to the gaps between CCD pixels is accounted for via random sampling. A ‘fill factor’, i.e., the ratio of total pixel area to total CCD area, is used to approximate the likelihood that an asteroid would fall between pixels. 2.5 Translating MEGASIM Orbits to Survey Pointings

Of the 11.2 million MEGASIM initialized orbits, only those stable for a minimum of 15,000 years ( ∼ similar-to \sim 368,287 ETA orbits) were propagated through the survey simulations. This propagation uses a maximum time step of 30 seconds, and is integrated to the exact time of each simulated LSST and ZTF observation time 14 14 14 The time of each simulated observation comes from OpSim for our simulated LSST surveys, and real observation times are taken for our simulated ZTF survey . If observation times are shorter than the 30 second timestep, then we integrate to the exact timestep of the exposure. To simulate how many MEGASIM ETAs could be detected in the LSST and ZTF surveys, we propagate the simulation forwards or backwards from t 0 subscript 𝑡 0 t_{0} =JD2459145.61625984 (the time that our surveys are initialized at) to the start date of the respective surveys; for LSST, forward propagation was done to sync with the start date of the LSST OpSim , and backward propagation was done to sync the Solar System with the beginning of the ZTF survey. The shifting of initialization resulted in on-sky errors of the order 10 − 6 superscript 10 6 10^{-6} ° in right ascension (R.A.) and 6 × 10 − 7 6 superscript 10 7 6\times 10^{-7} ° in declination (Dec.). A maximum error in pointing of 2.25 arcseconds in R.A. and Dec. occurred over the course of the entire simulation. Considering the effective field-of-view diameters of 1.75° for LSST and 3.4° for ZTF, the deviations between the simulated and true positions do not significantly affect results.

Once the MEGASIM simulated Solar System reaches the timestamp of a given survey pointing, a check is done to determine which ETAs are at an R.A. and Dec. within the observations field-of-view. To simplify this check, a circle is used to approximate the field-of-view of LSST and a square for ZTF. The effective circle for LSST is taken to be of radius 1.75°, and for ZTF, the square has side lengths of 3.428° and an area of 47 sq degrees. ETAs found within a survey’s field-of-view are then cataloged. A further check is then done to determine if the ETA in the field-of-view is bright enough to be detected by the telescope. If the ETA apparent magnitude is brighter than the 5 σ 𝜎 \sigma sensitivity for that exposure, it is cataloged as a detection.

2.6 Generating Detection Statistics

To place statistical weights on ETA detections in the LSST and ZTF surveys, detections in each survey simulation were recalculated by assigning all ETAs a new magnitude ( H 𝐻 H ), diameter ( D ast subscript 𝐷 ast D_{\text{ast}} ), and albedo ( p v subscript 𝑝 𝑣 p_{v} ). New ETA properties are obtained via resampling the H 𝐻 H and p v subscript 𝑝 𝑣 p_{v} distributions laid out in § 2.2 , enabling ETA resampling and detection determination in post without needing to re-simulate all of the ETA trajectories. This resampling was completed 1000 times for each LSST Opsim and ZTF pointing. In each step of the resampling, a unique detection list was created following the procedure described above.

3.1 MEGASIM coordinates

4.4 The Yarkovsky Effect and ETA Detections

In the MEGASIM, only gravitational forces were used to propagate the ETA orbits. Here we estimate the number of gravitationally stable ETAs that may be driven unstable by non-gravitational effects. The dominant additional force to consider is the Yarkovsky Effect (e.g., Chesley et al., 2003 ) , which causes asteroids to rotate and increase their semi-major axis due to Solar radiation pressure. Simulations of ETA orbits with the Yarkovsky Effect included were done by Zhou et al. ( 2019 ) . The Yarkovsky Effect’s strength depends on the shape, albedo distribution, surface conductivity, spin, and density of a given asteroid. The additional force from the Yarkovsky Effect was found to remove ETAs within 1 Gyr , for ETAs with diameters smaller than 130 m eter or 90 m eter, for prograde and retrograde rotation, respectively (Zhou et al., 2019 ) .

To estimate the changes to our survey completeness curves that the Yarkovsky Effect would impose, a cut-off on the simulated ETA population was made using both the lifetime and diameter of a given ETA. This is due to the fact that our detected ETAs are either too large (i.e., the diameter of ETAs just large enough to be detected by LSST are also just above the diameter for which the Yarkovsky effect will produce orbital instability) , or have not been around long enough for the Yarkovsky Effect to manifest instability (i.e. Gyr timescales). 5 Conclusion

Figure 17: Cumulative upper limit on ETA populations for the three surveys. The dotted lines show the 68% CI.

The work presented here can be categorized into three areas. 1) The spatial distributions of MEGASIM ETA trajectories, 2) the detectability of MEGASIM ETAs by LSST and ZTF, and 3) an upper limit of an actual ETA population assuming a null detection.

5.1 ETA S patial D istributions

When viewing L4 ETAs from Earth, the highest on-sky concentration is found 6.5° closer to the Sun than the L4 Lagrange point. 30% of ETA trajectories are found within 45-60° S olar elongation and another 30% are found beyond 60°. 70% of all ETAs exist within 5° of the ecliptic plane across all longitudes. Detection of ETAs from the ground is challenging inside L4 , and d etections cannot be made at all times of the year due to the Earth’s axial tilt (Whiteley & Tholen, 1998 ) . O bservations require longer exposure times due to higher sky brightness at twilight , and higher airmass than typical observations at opposition.

The distribution of ETAs as viewed looking down the pole of the ecliptic plane shows a toroidal volume that tapers near the Earth and L3 Lagrange point. ETAs can librate in as far as the orbit of Venus , and out as far as 1.3 AU, especially if they are only stable for short periods of time. ETAs that are stable longer mostly stay within 0.98 - 1.02 AU of the Sun , and i n the ecliptic plane, the highest density of ETAs oscillates over a considerable distance. High densities occur along a ridge spanning 45° leading Earth’s orbit to 90° , with the peak within a few degrees of the L4 point ( if averaged over time ) . ETAs have long libration periods (decades to millennia), so the time it takes to oscillate from behind the Sun to closer toward the Earth could fall completely outside of the survey observation window, meaning that modern surveys for observing the ETA population may not be feasible. .

As the lifetime of ETAs increases, the region that surviving ETAs traverse tightens onto the Earth’s orbit. ETAs that are stable over long periods of time do not approach Earth or L3 as closely , and remain in a tighter bunch around L4. This is one reason for fewer detections of longer-term stable ETAs in Figure 16 , even for ZTF and the LSST twilight survey, which are notionally designed for such low Solar elongation observations.

The on-sky location where ETAs are brightest does not correlate to the most likely position on the sky. The brightest on-sky areas are found at S olar elongations outside of L4 , at angles of 60° to 75°. The brightest location is on the ecliptic, but elevated brightness regions spread beyond L4 to latitudes of ± 20 plus-or-minus 20 \pm 20 . This is apparent in Figure   2 as a flaring yellow regime to the right of L4, and is due to the improved observing geometry.

5.2 Ability of LSST and ZTF to D etect ETAs

Due to the latitude that the telescopes for ZTF and LSST are located, the time of year in which each can detect L4 ETAs differs (see Figure   9 ). ZTF finds most ETAs from June to December , and LSST from January to June. In our simulations, ZTF was unsuccessful at detecting the vast majority of the ETA population , due to limited photometric sensitivity ( H < 19 𝐻 19 H<19 for most detections). Of the ZTF detections, only 4 unique ETAs were observed. Though, due to the field - of - view of the ZTF telescope , repeat detections are on the order of thousands, which indicates that any detected ETA by ZTF would be very well characterized and tracked.

Both the LSST baseline and twilight survey recovered the same number of unique ETAs, 188. However, t he twilight survey was able to make several thousand repeat detections, with 4 of the asteroids observed over 10,000 times. These results show that a twilight survey will not increase the likelihood of individual ETA detections drastically compared to the baseline survey. Though, a twilight survey would provide us with several orders of magnitude more observations of select ETAs. Observing asteroids on the order of thousands to tens of thousands of times is unprecedented and would be useful for constraining characteriz ation of individual asteroids.

5.3 The P ossible ETA P opulation

ZTF did not have a statistically significant number of detections to provide meaningful constraints on the upper limit of the ETA population , for any size range. That is, at magnitude H < 𝐻 absent H< 18, the probability that an ETA exists become so low that ZTF’s observing cadence will not detect the ETA frequently enough to count as an observed ETA. However, both the LSST baseline and twilight surveys provided enough detections to estimate an upper limit for ETAs , of Myr up to Gyr stability times. Due to the Yarkovsky Effect, C-type and S-type ETAs of absolute magnitudes greater than H = 22 𝐻 22 H=22 and H = 24 𝐻 24 H=24 , respectively, will be driven unstable within 1 Gyr. The rarity of ETAs greater than 100 m eters in diameter , paired with the fact that smaller asteroids will be driven unstable, our results indicate that a null detection by LSST will mean that there are no remaining Gyr-stable or primordial ETA populations. On the other hand, the existing ETA discoveries are indicative of a small and transient population. A null detection in LSST will restrict that population to tens of objects larger than 100 meters.
