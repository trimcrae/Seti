\begin{abstract}
LUX-ZEPLIN has reported one event consistent with a \EventEnergy\keV nuclear recoil in
\Exposure\,tonne-years, recorded on \EventDate, in an analysis window extended to
reach effective-field-theory and inelastic dark-matter spectra.  The follow-up
literature agrees that an endothermic recoil with a mass splitting $\delta$ close to
the kinematic limit set by the halo escape speed is the natural dark-matter reading.
We take that reading at its word: a recoil at the kinematic edge is carried by the
few tens of \si{km/s} of the velocity distribution just below $\vesc+\vlab$, i.e.\ by
the part of the local dark matter that Gaia has measured through halo stars and
that the Large Magellanic Cloud is predicted to dominate.  We compute the joint
likelihood of the event's energy \emph{and date} as a function of $(m_\chi,\delta)$
for the sharp-cut Maxwellian used by the collaboration, for soft and power-law
tails, for the published escape-speed measurements integrated over their
uncertainties, and for an LMC-boosted tail.  \TODO{results sentence: delta
posterior per measurement; timing Bayes factor; LMC phase; predictions}.  The
date alone carries a Bayes factor of \TimingBFbest against a constant-rate
background at the best-fit splitting, and the same models predict the seasonal
window within which the next such events must fall.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The LUX-ZEPLIN collaboration has re-searched its 220 live-day WS2024 exposure
(27 March 2023 to 1 April 2024, 4.71~t fiducial, \Exposure\,tonne-years) in a
nuclear-recoil window extended to $\approx270$\keV, motivated by non-relativistic
effective-field-theory operators and inelastic scattering, both of which move recoil
spectra to energies where the spin-independent signal has long vanished
\citep{LZ2026extended}.  One event survives every selection: at 21:22:39~UTC on
16~June~2023, a single scatter reconstructed as a \EventEnergy\,$\pm$\,23\,(stat)\,$\pm$\,23\,(sys)\keV
nuclear recoil, 1.5$\sigma$ below the median of the nuclear-recoil band and 6.7$\sigma$
below the electron-recoil band, in a region where the known backgrounds sum to
$0.011$ events.  The collaboration reports a global significance of 2.6$\sigma$ and
local significances up to 3.4$\sigma$ for momentum-dependent operators and for
endothermic scattering with splittings of 250--350\keV at masses above 200\,GeV.

Nineteen papers appeared within a week.  Their common conclusion, reached from
different model-building directions
\citep{Freese2026higgsino,DiMauro2026edge,McCabe2026seasonal}, is that the natural
dark-matter reading is an endothermic recoil, $\chi N\to\chi^\ast N$ with
$m_{\chi^\ast}-m_\chi=\delta$, and that the observed energy pushes $\delta$ to within a
few tens of keV of the largest value the halo can supply.  \citet{McCabe2026seasonal}
puts it precisely: at 1\,TeV the fitted splittings lie at 370--400\keV, where
``the entire signal is supplied by dark matter lying within 44\kms of the escape
speed, a sliver of the velocity distribution and the part of the local halo that is
most uncertain'', and leaves the dependence on that part ``for future work''.
\citet{DiMauro2026edge} shows that such a signal would be concentrated in a few weeks
around the annual maximum of the laboratory's speed through the halo and declines to
assign the 16~June date a probability without the detector's live-time distribution.
The high-energy sideband above the window, which LZ uses to validate a background
model and finds empty, is turned into an upper bound on $\delta$ for the Higgsino by
\citet{HiggsinoSideband2026}, who also add a Large-Magellanic-Cloud sub-component and
scan the escape speed upward from the conventional 544\kms.

This paper takes the astrophysical half of that programme seriously.  If the event is
a recoil at the kinematic edge, it is a measurement of the fastest dark matter in the
solar neighbourhood, and three facts about that population are available from
astronomy rather than from convention.  First, the local escape speed has been
measured from halo stars, and most measurements sit \emph{below} 544\kms:
$484.6^{+17.8}_{-7.4}$ \citep{NecibLin2022vesc}, $497\pm8$
\citep{Koppelmanvesc}, $521^{+46}_{-30}$ \citep{Roche2024vesc} and
$528^{+24}_{-25}$\kms \citep{Deason2019vesc}, against $580\pm63$\kms
\citep{Monari2018vesc} and the RAVE value of 544\kms adopted by
\citet{Baxter2021conventions}.  Second, the shape of the tail near the escape speed
is not a sharp cut: the stellar fits are power laws $(\vesc-v)^k$ whose slope is
degenerate with $\vesc$ itself.  Third, simulations of a Milky Way with an LMC on
first infall place a small, fast population above the nominal escape speed
\citep{Besla2019lmc,SmithOrlik2023lmc}.  We evaluate, for every such description of
the tail, the same three-factor likelihood of the one event: its energy, its date
against the live time of the run, and the empty sideband.  The result is a posterior
on $\delta$ for each escape-speed measurement, an evidence for each halo model given
the event, and, read the other way, the event's own likelihood for the local escape
speed.

\section{Kinematics of a recoil at the edge}
For endothermic scattering $\chi N\to\chi^\ast N$ with $m_{\chi^\ast}-m_\chi=\delta\ge0$,
the minimum speed for a recoil of energy $E_R$ off a nucleus of mass $m_N$ is
\begin{equation}
  \vmin(E_R)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2m_NE_R}}\left(\frac{m_NE_R}{\mu}+\delta\right),
\end{equation}
with $\mu$ the reduced mass; it is smallest, $\sqrt{2\delta/\mu}$, at
$E_R^\ast=\mu\delta/m_N$.  For a heavy state scattering on xenon the event's
energy fixes $\vmin(\EventEnergy\keV)=\VminElastic\kms$ at $\delta=0$ and adds
$\delta/\sqrt{2m_NE_R}\simeq\VminPerHundredKeV\kms$ per \SI{100}{keV} of splitting.
The lab frame sees no particle faster than $\vesc+\vlab(t)$, so for a given halo the
largest splitting compatible with the event on its date is
$\delta_{\max}=\DeltaMaxLZ\keV$ (\SI{1}{TeV}, $\vesc=\VescLZ\kms$).  Because $\vlab$
changes by $\pm\VlabAmp\kms$ through the year, there is a band of splittings for
which the event is kinematically possible only within a window of dates around
1~June: the \emph{reachability calendar} (Fig.~\ref{fig:calendar}).  The event's
date, \EventDate, is \DaysAfterPeak days after the maximum of $\vlab$.

\section{Halo models and the shape of the tail}
Every velocity distribution is specified in the Galactic rest frame and boosted by
$\vlab(t)=\mathbf v_{\rm LSR}+\mathbf v_\odot+\mathbf v_\oplus(t)$, with
$v_0=238\kms$, the \citet{Schoenrich2010} peculiar velocity and the ecliptic vectors of
\citet{McCabe2014earth}; $|\vlab|$ peaks on 1~June at 266.4\kms and troughs on
1~December at 237\kms, and was 266.0\kms on the date of the event.  The rate needs
only the inverse-speed integral $g(\vmin,t)=\int_{v>\vmin}f_{\rm lab}(\mathbf v,t)/v\,d^3v$.

\emph{Sharp cut.}  The standard halo model: a Maxwellian truncated at $\vesc$ in the
Galactic frame.  Its lab-frame speed distribution is elementary (the angular integral
over the spherical cap inside the escape sphere is a difference of two exponentials),
and we verify the numerical $g$ against the closed form of \citet{Savage2006} to
$5\times10^{-3}$.

\emph{Soft cut and power-law tail.}  The sharp cut is an artefact of the model.  The
stellar fits from which $\vesc$ is inferred use $f(v)\propto(\vesc-v)^k$ near the edge,
with $k$ between 1 and 3 and strongly degenerate with $\vesc$
\citep{Deason2019vesc,Koppelmanvesc}; we implement that tail joined
continuously to the Maxwellian at $0.8\vesc$, and the King-like soft cut
$\exp(-v^2/v_0^2)-\exp(-\vesc^2/v_0^2)$ as an intermediate case.  For anisotropic
components (the Sausage of SHM$^{++}$, \citealt{Evans2019shmpp}) and for the
LMC component below, the escape-sphere cap is integrated exactly: the
Gauss--Legendre nodes in $\cos\theta$ are mapped onto the allowed sub-interval at
each speed, so the hard edge never falls between nodes.

\emph{The LMC.}  Following \citet{HiggsinoSideband2026}, who summarise
\citet{Besla2019lmc} and \citet{SmithOrlik2023lmc}, we add a Gaussian of dispersion
100\kms boosted to $|\mathbf v_b|=570\kms$ along the LMC's velocity direction (135$^\circ$
from the Sun's), truncated at $|\mathbf v-\mathbf v_b|<200\kms$, with density
fraction $w=0.26\%$ (the present-day value of the re-simulated Auriga halo) and
$0.6\%$.  In June this component reaches 980\kms in the laboratory.  Because the LMC
moves against Galactic rotation, its particles arrive from nearly the same direction
as the halo wind and are fastest in the same weeks \citep{Besla2019lmc}, so the date
of the event does not separate the two; the sideband does.

Figure~\ref{fig:tails} shows $f_{\rm lab}(v)$ on 16~June~2023 for these models with
$\vmin(248\keV;\delta)$ marked for a 1\,TeV state.  At $\delta=380$\keV the density at
$\vmin$ differs by two orders of magnitude between the sharp cut and a $k=2.5$ tail,
and by a factor of a few between $\vesc=528$ and 544\kms: the answer to ``which
splitting?'' is set by the tail, not by the detector.

\section{The likelihood of one event}
For an expected count $\mu=\sigma_n\,\hat\mu$ of signal events in the window, one
observed at energy $E_{\rm obs}$ and date $t_{\rm obs}$, and none in the sideband, the
extended likelihood is
$\mathcal L=e^{-\mu}\,\mu\,p(E_{\rm obs},t_{\rm obs})\,e^{-\mu_{\rm SB}}$, with
$p(E,t)=R(E,t)L(t)/\int\!\!\int R L\,dE\,dt$ the normalised density of a signal event in
energy and live time.  Because the search-window density and the sideband count are
both proportional to $\sigma_n$, the likelihood factorises as
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal L(m_\chi,\delta,\sigma_n)\propto e^{-\mu}\mu\times
  \underbrace{\frac{R(t_{\rm obs})}{\langle R\rangle_{\rm live}}}_{\text{date}}\times
  \underbrace{p(E_{\rm obs}\,|\,t_{\rm obs})}_{\text{energy}}\times
  \underbrace{\exp\!\left[-\mu\,\frac{R_{\rm SB}}{R}\right]}_{\text{sideband}},
  \label{eq:like}
\end{equation}
where $R(t)$ is the efficiency-weighted rate in the 5.4--270\keV window,
$\langle R\rangle_{\rm live}$ its average over the live time (uniform between 27~March
2023 and 1~April~2024, the only information the paper gives), $p(E_{\rm obs}|t_{\rm obs})$
the recoil spectrum on the date of the event after the $\sqrt E$ resolution anchored at
$\pm23$\keV and the $\pm23$\keV energy-scale systematic marginalised as a shift, and
$R_{\rm SB}/R$ the ratio of the rate in the empty sideband (taken as 350--600\keV with
the plateau efficiency, as \citealt{HiggsinoSideband2026}) to the window rate.  The date
factor is unity for an unmodulated signal, grows with the modulation amplitude and is
zero for a model whose rate vanishes on 16~June; the sideband factor is
$e^{-N_{\rm SB}}$ with $N_{\rm SB}$ the sideband count per window event.  We marginalise
$\sigma_n$ with a log-uniform prior up to a ceiling of $10^{-37}$\,cm$^2$, which turns
$e^{-\mu}\mu$ into $1-e^{-\mu_{\max}}$: models that cannot make one event at any allowed
cross section are penalised, while the profile alone is flat in $\sigma_n$ and runs to
the grid edge where one event would need $10^{-34}$\,cm$^2$.  Figure~\ref{fig:scan}
shows the date and energy factors against $\delta$.

The energy factor is what every published fit uses.  The date factor is the one
\citet{McCabe2026seasonal} includes and \citet{DiMauro2026edge} declines; we take the
uniform live time as the collaboration's stated span implies; the factor at
$\delta=370$\keV ranges from \TimingMaskMin to \TimingMaskMax across the live-time
masks of Section~\ref{sec:sys}, against 3.4 for the uniform one.  The sideband factor is that of
\citet{HiggsinoSideband2026} and \citet{DentNewstead2026}: for a heavy state the
spectral peak sits at $E^\ast=\mu\delta/m_N$, which for $\delta\gtrsim300$\keV lies
\emph{above} the window, so the observed 248\keV is on the rising edge and the empty
sideband bounds $\delta$ from above.  With all three factors the splitting is pinned
from both sides: from below by the energy, from above by the sideband, and the date
rewards the models in between that are most seasonal.

\section{Results}
\subsection{The splitting, and what sets it}
Under the conventional halo ($v_0=238$, $\vesc=544\kms$, sharp cut) the marginal
posterior for a 1\,TeV state peaks at $\delta=\DeltaBestShmLz$\keV with a one-sigma
interval of \DeltaOneShmLz\keV.  The energy factor alone would run to the kinematic
edge at 387\keV, as in every published fit; the sideband factor is what stops it,
because for $\delta\gtrsim330$\keV the spectral peak $E^\ast=\mu\delta/m_N$ crosses
into the empty 350--600\keV band (\BestSideband sideband events per window event at
the posterior maximum, 2.4 at 370\keV, 10 at 380\keV).  Including the sideband
therefore moves the one-sigma splitting from the 370--400\keV of
\citet{McCabe2026seasonal} to \DeltaOneShmLz\keV; our sideband count at
$\delta=377$\keV, \SidebandAtEdge per window event, agrees with the 4.9 of
\citet{HiggsinoSideband2026} to the accuracy their digitised efficiency and our
described one allow.

At a fixed escape speed the posterior maximum moves with it: \DeltaBestShmVescLow\keV
(\DeltaOneShmVescLow) at $\vesc=500\kms$, \DeltaBestShmVescHigh\keV
(\DeltaOneShmVescHigh) at 600\kms, \DeltaBestShmPp\keV (\DeltaOneShmPp) with the
Sausage of SHM$^{++}$ at 528\kms.  Integrating over each published measurement
instead (Fig.~\ref{fig:vesc}) shows what is robust and what is not.  The mode is
\DeltaModeKoppelman\keV for \citet{Koppelmanvesc},
\DeltaModeNecib\keV for \citet{NecibLin2022vesc},
\DeltaModeDeason\keV for \citet{Deason2019vesc},
\DeltaModeRoche\keV for \citet{Roche2024vesc} and
\DeltaModeMonari\keV for \citet{Monari2018vesc}: the same value, because
for every halo it is the point where the spectral peak reaches the sideband.  The
68\,\% intervals share a long lower tail (\DeltaSixtyEightKoppelman\keV,
\DeltaSixtyEightDeason\keV, \DeltaSixtyEightMonari\keV), set by
the slowly rising energy factor and the log-uniform cross-section prior.  What the
escape speed governs is the upper tail: the 95\,\% upper bound on $\delta$ at 1\,TeV
is \DeltaNinetyFiveUpperKoppelman\keV for \citet{Koppelmanvesc},
\DeltaNinetyFiveUpperNecib\keV for \citet{NecibLin2022vesc},
\DeltaNinetyFiveUpperDeason\keV for \citet{Deason2019vesc},
\DeltaNinetyFiveUpperRoche\keV for \citet{Roche2024vesc},
\DeltaNinetyFiveUpperRave\keV for the RAVE value and
\DeltaNinetyFiveUpperMonari\keV for \citet{Monari2018vesc}.  A model that
needs $\delta\gtrsim370$\keV at 1\,TeV to suppress a fixed cross section, as the
thermal Higgsino does through $Z$ exchange \citep{HiggsinoSideband2026}, is therefore
outside the 95\,\% region for the two precise Gaia measurements and inside it only for
the loosest; the independent exclusion of the same model by solar capture
\citep{PospelovRamani2026} does not depend on the halo at all.

\subsection{The date}
On 16~June~2023 the laboratory moved through the halo 0.4\kms below its annual
maximum.  For the models the energy and sideband select, the date factor
$R(t_{\rm obs})/\langle R\rangle_{\rm live}$ is \TimingBFbest at the posterior maximum:
the rate on the day of the event was that much larger than its average over the
run, because the signal is on for only part of the year (Fig.~\ref{fig:scan}).  It is
1.0 for elastic scattering, whose window rate peaks in December, and it depends on
the unknown live-time mask by \TimingMaskMin--\TimingMaskMax at $\delta=370$\keV
(Section~\ref{sec:sys}).  The pure-kinematics version of the same statement is the
reachability calendar (Fig.~\ref{fig:calendar}): for a 1\,TeV state under the
conventional halo a 248\keV recoil is possible all year for $\delta\le360$\keV,
between 30~January and 1~October for 370\keV, between 22~March and 12~August for
380\keV, between 21~April and 12~July for 385\keV, and never for $\delta\ge390$\keV.
The muon flux at the Davis campus modulates at the per-cent level with a July maximum
and cannot supply a comparable factor; the collaboration's own bound on muon-induced
neutrons is $4.6\times10^{-4}$ events.

\subsection{Halo models compared}
Marginalised over mass (log-uniform over 200\,GeV--10\,TeV), splitting (uniform to
480\keV) and cross section (log-uniform to $10^{-37}$\,cm$^2$), the evidences of the
halo models given the event differ by less than a factor of two
(Fig.~\ref{fig:evidence}): \EvidShmLz for the conventional halo, \EvidShmPp for
SHM$^{++}$, \EvidShmVescLow and \EvidShmVescHigh for $\vesc=500$ and 600\kms, and
\EvidShmLmcLow and \EvidShmLmcHigh for the LMC admixtures, relative to the best.
A single event cannot rank the tails: a larger escape speed is compensated by a
larger splitting, and the three factors are satisfied at the shifted edge.  The
LMC admixture opens a qualitatively different region rather than a better fit: a
lighter state (200\,GeV) with $\delta\simeq410$\keV, whose spectrum peaks inside the
window, puts nothing in the sideband and is on only in the weeks when the
LMC-borne particles are fastest (date factor 6).  It needs a per-nucleon cross
section of $4.5\times10^{-38}$\,cm$^2$ and exists only because the prior admits it;
with a Higgsino-like ceiling of $10^{-39}$\,cm$^2$ it is gone.

\subsection{The escape speed from the event}
Read the other way, with a flat prior on $\vesc$ between 440 and 640\kms and
$m_\chi$, $\delta$, $\sigma_n$ marginalised, the event's own likelihood of the local
escape speed (Fig.~\ref{fig:vesc_inverse}) is flat: it varies by $\pm5\,\%$ across the
range, its median (\VescMedian\kms) and 68\,\% interval (\VescSixtyEight\kms) are
those of the prior, and the posteriors conditioned on each stellar measurement
reproduce that measurement.  The reason is the degeneracy of the previous section:
whatever the escape speed, there is a splitting at which the energy, the date and the
sideband are all satisfied.  A single event at the kinematic edge is a measurement of
$\delta$ given the halo, not of the halo; the halo enters as a systematic on
$\delta$, of order 50\keV at 1\,TeV across the published measurements, and a
second event with a different energy or date is what would begin to lift it.

\subsection{Other experiments}
No published search other than LZ's has a nuclear-recoil window that reaches
248\keV with a tonne-scale exposure: XENON100's effective-field-theory search
stops at 240\keV with 7.6\,kg-years, and the XENONnT and PandaX-4T results end
below 100\keV.  LZ's own first run used the same 3--600\,phd S1$c$ range in its
effective-field-theory search \citep{LZ2024eftSR1} but ran from 23~December~2021 to
12~May~2022, the months when an edge signal is weakest; at the posterior maxima
its sensitivity per unit exposure was \WsTwentyTwoRelShmVescLow--\WsTwentyTwoRelShmVescHigh
of WS2024's, so its null result (0.9\,t\,yr, no candidate) is a weaker constraint
than its exposure suggests but not an absent one.  The effect grows steeply toward
the edge: within 20\keV of $\delta_{\max}$ a December--May run tests nothing, which
is the consideration for the confirmation exposures now being planned.

\section{Predictions}
For each halo model the same likelihood fixes, up to the Poisson uncertainty of one
count, when and where the next events must come.  Under the conventional halo at
its posterior maximum ($\delta=\DeltaBestShmLz$\keV, 1\,TeV) the window rate is
above one tenth of its annual peak from \SeasonStartShmLz to \SeasonEndShmLz and
peaks on 31~May; 90\,\% of the recoils on the peak date fall between
\ERangeShmLz\keV, a quarter of them above the 270\keV window edge; and one event
per 2.84\,t\,yr implies \NThousandShmLz window events in LZ's projected 1000 live
days with \NSbThousandShmLz in the 350--600\keV sideband if the efficiency plateau
extends.  With $\vesc=500\kms$ the season contracts to
\SeasonStartShmVescLow--\SeasonEndShmVescLow and the sideband yield to
\NSbThousandShmVescLow; with the LMC admixture the season lengthens (the fast
component is on all year) and the sideband yield is \NSbThousandShmLmcLow.  Two or
three further events, with their dates and energies, would separate the halo models
that one event cannot: the sideband count, the summer concentration and the
presence of recoils above 300\keV are the discriminants.

\section{Systematics}\label{sec:sys}
\emph{Live time.}  LZ reports 220 live days between 27~March 2023 and 1~April~2024
and no calendar.  With the live time concentrated after 1~June the date factor at
370\keV rises to 4.8; ending on 31~December it falls to 2.7; halving the summer duty
gives 4.3, halving the winter duty 2.8; removing the calibration weeks around the
event changes it by 4\,\%.  \emph{Energy scale.}  The $\pm23$\keV systematic is
marginalised as a shift; it moves $\vmin(248\keV)$ by $\pm28\kms$ at fixed $\delta$,
comparable to the spread of the escape-speed measurements.  \emph{Efficiency and
sideband edges.}  We use the paper's description of Fig.~S2 (96\,\% plateau from
14 to 250\keV, 50\,\% at 269.9\keV) and a sideband of 350--600\keV at plateau
efficiency; \citet{HiggsinoSideband2026} find the sideband count varies by
$\pm40\,\%$ under the S1$c$-to-energy mapping.  \emph{Form factor.}  Helm, with the
second zero at 278\keV for Xe-131 just above the event; a Vietze form factor lowers
the sideband count by 25\,\% in \citet{HiggsinoSideband2026}.  \emph{Isotopes.}  The
Xe-136 threshold lies 20\kms below Xe-129's; the last 20\kms of every edge is
carried by the heavy isotopes.

\section{Conclusions}
If LZ230616 is an endothermic recoil, its energy, its date and the empty sideband
above it together pin the splitting from both sides, and the value they pin it to is
set by the local escape speed: \DeltaOneShmLz\keV at 1\,TeV for the conventional
544\kms, some 30\keV lower for the Gaia measurements that most analyses now favour,
and higher only with the LMC's fast population.  One event cannot say which halo is
right; the next ones, with their dates, can.
