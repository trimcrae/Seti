Abstract

Using N-body simulation results from the MEGASIM dataset, we present spatial distributions of Earth Trojan Asteroids and assess the detectability of the population in current and next-generation ground-based astronomical surveys (Yeager & Golovich, 2022a). Our high-fidelity Earth Trojan Asteroid (ETA) distribution maps show never-before-seen high-resolution spatial features that evolve over timescales up to 1 Gyr. The simulation was synchronized to start times and timelines of two observational astronomy surveys, 1) the Vera C. Rubin Observatory’s Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) and 2) the Zwicky Transient Facility (ZTF). We calculate upper limits for the number of ETAs potentially observable with both the ZTF and LSST surveys. Due to the Yarkovsky Effect, we find no stable ETAs on billion year timescales likely to be detected by any ETA survey, as no C-type or S-type ETAs (with H<22𝐻22H<22 and H<24𝐻24H<24, respectively) are likely to be stable on billion year timescales, and ETAs large enough to remain stable on billion year timescales are very rare relative to the rest of the ETA population. We find that a twilight ETA survey will not drastically increase the likelihood of individual ETA detection, but would provide orders of magnitude more observations of select ETA populations. The null detection to date from ZTF restricts the potential ETA population to hundreds of objects larger than 100 meters (at H≈22𝐻22H\approx 22, Lifset et al., 2021), while a null detection by LSST will further restrict the ETA population to tens of objects larger than 100 meters.

1 Introduction

Trojan asteroids111The term ‘Trojan’ generally refers to objects co-orbiting the Sun with Jupiter; however, given the general nature of these minor bodies across the Solar System, we use the term ‘Trojan’ generically to refer to which planet they co-orbit the Sun with. are a class of co-orbiting objects that librate about the L4 or L5 Lagrangian points within a planet’s orbit. Mapping the distributions of possible Earth Trojan Asteroids (ETAs) is important to guide observational searches, as ETAs may answer pressing science questions about how our Solar System and Moon were formed. Librations of ETAs can carry an asteroid nearly the entire distance between the planet they are co-orbiting with, and its L3 Lagrange point on the far side of the orbit behind the Sun. In a rotating reference frame, the gravitational potential lines look like tadpoles, where these tadpole orbits occupy a half-toroidal region, which includes either the L4 or L5 point within. The region of the sky where ETAs potentially exist is difficult to observe due to the low Solar elongation. The geometry also tends to have small reflection angles, making observations even more difficult from Earth. However, synoptic surveys such as those carried out by the Zwicky Transient Facility (ZTF, Bellm et al., 2018) and the Vera C. Rubin Observatory (LSST Science Collaboration et al., 2009; Jones et al., 2020) could still overcome the difficult observing conditions using numerous observations at the periphery of the ETA regime. Twilight surveys, in particular, could be powerful probes into the population of ETAs of interest; ZTF has been carrying out a twilight survey for years (see e.g., Ye et al., 2020), and there is current discussion on whether or not the Rubin Observatory’s Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) will or will not have a dedicated twilight survey (Seaman et al., 2018).

1.1 Scientific Impact of ETAs

There are several potential sourcing mechanisms for Trojan asteroids: 1) they may be formed in the early proto-planetary disk near a planet’s L4 or L5 Lagrange point (Montesinos et al., 2020), 2) they may be captured debris produced from major planetary impacts, and 3) they may be sourced from perturbed populations of asteroids elsewhere in the Solar System (either during planetary migration or from ejected asteroids from the main-asteroid belt). The first two mechanisms would have occurred early in the formation of the Solar System, and we consider these to be “primordial” in nature. For ETAs, primordial sourcing through the impact hypothesis may also explain lunar formation. The giant impact hypothesis has been proposed, suggesting that a Mars-sized proto-planet could have formed in a stable orbit among debris, at either Earth’s L4 or L5 Lagrange point, and collided with proto-Earth from a 1 AU orbit in the solar nebula to form the Moon (Belbruno & Gott, 2005). It is hypothesized that a debris cloud from such a collision could have provided a primordial source of ETAs (Canup, 2004). If found, such a population of asteroids would offer a unique lens into the evolution of the early Solar System and the Earth-Moon system. The existence of primordial ETAs would offer ideal low-Δ​vΔ𝑣\Delta v targets for rendezvous missions to study the early inner Solar System, especially the Earth-Moon system (Malhotra, 2019). NASA’s Lucy mission will soon begin its 12-year journey to several families of Jupiter Trojans, which will begin to answer many of the scientific questions we have discussed (Zhou et al., 2020). While the Lucy mission is focused on primordial Jupiter Trojans, it may shed light on how scientifically rich these objects are, validating that future Earth Trojan missions will be important in understanding our Solar System.

1.2 Observational ETA Searches

Observers have searched for ETAs with a few relatively small astronomical twilight surveys (Whiteley & Tholen, 1998; Connors et al., 2000; Markwardt et al., 2020; Lifset et al., 2021). Markwardt et al. (2020) and Lifset et al. (2021) were able to use a null detection to place competitive upper limits on the ETA population, predicting an L4 ETA population(NE​Tsubscript𝑁𝐸𝑇{N}_{ET}) of NE​T<1subscript𝑁𝐸𝑇1{N}_{ET}<1 for absolute magnitude H=14𝐻14H=14, NE​T<10subscript𝑁𝐸𝑇10{N}_{ET}<10 for H=16𝐻16H=16, and NE​T<938subscript𝑁𝐸𝑇938{N}_{ET}<938 for H=22𝐻22H=22. Efforts have been proposed on how to best observe the ETA population utilizing Solar System exploration missions (Cambioni et al., 2018; Yoshikawa et al., 2018; Malhotra, 2019), ground-based telescopes (Todd et al., 2012, 2014; Seaman et al., 2018; Ye et al., 2020), and a near-Earth object (NEO) Surveyor Mainzer et al. (2021) which is planned to launch this decade to search for potentially hazardous asteroids, including ETAs.

Currently, there are two known ETAs: 2010 T​K7𝑇subscript𝐾7TK_{7} and 2020 X​L5𝑋subscript𝐿5XL_{5} (Connors et al., 2011; de la Fuente Marcos & de la Fuente Marcos, 2021)222Both known ETAs are at Earth’s L4 Lagrange point. None have been found at L5.. Both ETAs librate far from the Lagrange points (i.e., they are transient in nature), and will only be co-orbiting with Earth for a short period of time compared to the age of the Solar System. Additionally, both known ETAs are on tadpole orbits that librate about Earth’s L4 Lagrange point, traversing much of the distance between Earth and Earth’s L3 Lagrange point behind the Sun. The orbits of both ETAs are stable on the order of thousands of years, with 2020 X​L5𝑋subscript𝐿5XL_{5} being the more stable of the two. 2020 X​L5𝑋subscript𝐿5XL_{5} was shown to likely have been captured in the 15th century and should remain stable for ∼similar-to\sim4000 years (Santana-Ros et al., 2022).

1.3 Previous ETA Simulations

We refer readers to our previous work in analyzing the MEGASIM results (described in more detail in §2) for an in-depth analysis on lifetime and stability of ETAs, including a detailed literature review (Yeager & Golovich, 2022a). Numerous additional ETA detectability studies have been carried out using numerical simulations, to understand and constrain ETA populations for guiding observers (e.g., Wiegert et al., 2000; Evans & Tabachnik, 2000; Morais & Morbidelli, 2002; Todd et al., 2012; Zhou et al., 2019; Lifset et al., 2021; Napier et al., 2022), and have typically centered around the questions of sourcing, lifetimes, stability zones, and observability of the stable population. Wiegert et al. (2000) produced spatial maps of time-averaged ETA positions from simulations of two synthetic populations of Earth Trojans by injecting co-orbiting Earth asteroids uniformly within the region between the L2 and L3 points. When viewing these ETA orbits from Earth, the resulting spatial density peak is a few degrees displaced toward the Sun from the L4 and L5 Lagrange points. Evans & Tabachnik (2000) and Morais & Morbidelli (2002) found stable zones with inclination between 10° and 45°, with a wide range of ecliptic longitudes encapsulating the L4 and L5 points, but spanning tens of degrees to either side of L4 or L5. Todd et al. (2012) modeled albedo, size, and orbital distributions of ETAs in an effort to define an ‘optimal’ search strategy for ETA detection, and based on their simulations, suggest a twilight observing campaign to search the wide swath of area that the ETAs in their simulation cover. The only known asteroid at the time of these simulations, 2010​T​K72010𝑇subscript𝐾72010TK_{7}, would have appeared in such a survey (Todd et al., 2012). While currently known ETAs exhibit orbital stability on the order of thousands of years, objects librating about the Earth’s L4 or L5 Lagrange points can be stable on the order of millions or billions of years (Yeager & Golovich, 2022a). An ETA’s ability to remain stable depends on several factors, e.g., spin rate, shape, size, albedo, density, and surface conductivity, of which play a role in non-gravitational forces such as the Yarkovsky Effect. Smaller ETAs experience larger effects from Solar radiation, thus driving them from the co-orbital regime more quickly. Simulations by (Zhou et al., 2019) find that only ETAs larger than ∼similar-to\sim100 meters remain stable for 1 Gyr. Lifset et al. (2021) placed upper limit constraints on the number of ETAs in L4, as described in §1.2.

In this paper, we study the MEGASIM data to assess the observability of ETAs in ongoing and upcoming synoptic surveys. In §2, we give a brief summary of the simulation details, and we describe our method of determining the flux of individual asteroids in the simulation. In §3, we present spatial distributions and discuss translating our simulation output into observational surveys. In §4, we present our results regarding the observability of ETAs in two synoptic surveys. Finally, in §5, we offer conclusions of our analysis.

2 MEGASIM

The Multitudinous Earth Greek Asteroid Simulation (MEGASIM), announced first in Yeager & Golovich (2022b), resulted in 11.2 million ETA orbits initialized on trajectories that span beyond the expected ETA stability regime around the L4 Lagrange point. The orbits have now been carried out to a maximum of 4.5 billion years, allowing a study of the longevity of ETA orbits.

2.1 Initialization and Parallelization

The MEGASIM data set consists of two distinct simulations, one using the WHFast integrator (Wisdom & Holman, 1991; Rein & Tamayo, 2015), and the other using the IAS15 (Rein & Spiegel, 2015) integrator (see Yeager & Golovich (2022a) for additional detail on these data sets). This work focuses on the WHFast data set, as the WHFast integrator is significantly faster than other symplectic integrators, enabling us to integrate to the full 4.5 billion year age of the Solar System. To achieve parallelization, the 11.2 million ETA trajectories were placed into batches of 500, resulting in 22,400 parallel simulated Solar Systems that were integrated forward in time. Each simulated Solar System is initialized with the Sun and eight planets (i.e., Mercury, Venus, Earth-Moon, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune), with initial planetary positions gathered from the JPL HORIZONS system333https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/horizons/ at an epoch of JD2459145.61625984 (Giorgini et al., 1996). We utilized the Catalyst, Quartz, and Ruby clusters managed by Livermore Computing at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory 444https://hpc.llnl.gov/hardware/platforms/ruby.

Each batch of asteroids were initialized with six orbital parameters selected from a random distribution, as described below. Each model was then integrated forward with REBOUND555https://github.com/hannorein/rebound(Rein & Liu, 2012). The integration time step was set to one-day, and position and velocity information for each simulation was output every 1000 years. Each ETA orbit was initialized with a true longitude (θ𝜃\theta) randomly and uniformly distributed between Earth and L3 (on the side of Earth’s orbit containing L4). The argument of pericenter and longitude of ascending node were randomly assigned values between 0 and 2​π2𝜋2\pi, assuming a uniform distribution. The semi-major axis (a𝑎a), eccentricity (e>0𝑒0e>0), and inclination (i𝑖i) were sampled from a three-dimensional Gaussian with mean (μ𝜇\mu) and covariance (ΣΣ\Sigma) given in Equation 1.

2.3 The LSST and ZTF Surveys

We use the LSST v2.2 baseline and twilight OpSim runs666https://www.lsst.org/scientists/simulations/opsim (‘lsst_no_twilight_neo_v2.2’ and ‘twi_neo_repeat4_riz_v2.2_10yrs’, respectively), as well as all ZTF observations spanning dates 2018-03-17 to 2022-06-01 (LSST project, 2022; Bellm et al., 2018), as the survey data for our analysis (see section 4). The number of observations in the LSST baseline OpSim was 2,259,570 and twilight 2,078,065. We filter down the ZTF data set to only include 30-second exposures taken by the Palomar P48 observing system, and we use both public survey data and private institutional partnership observational data. The resulting dataset consists of 706,459 30-second observations.

2.4 Estimating Survey Losses

To be considered a detection, one of two conditions must be satisfied: 1) three pairs of observations (at minimum) are required within a month-long rolling window, or 2) two observations are required within one hour of one another. These conditions are reasonable for orbit fitting between observations (e.g., Jedicke, 2006; Denneau et al., 2009) and are to be used by LSST (Jones et al., 2018). Survey detections can be lost from non-ideal conditions such as observation linking, trailing losses from streaking across a detector (i.e., the CCD), and chip gaps between pixels on the detector. Loss of detection due to the gaps between CCD pixels is accounted for via random sampling. A ‘fill factor’, i.e., the ratio of total pixel area to total CCD area, is used to approximate the likelihood that an asteroid would fall between pixels. The fill factor for LSST is 0.877, and for ZTF is 0.875 (Jones et al., 2018; Bellm et al., 2018)

To estimate trailing losses (i.e., the loss in flux from streaking over the detector) due to asteroid streaking over an exposure, we run an exposure time calculator using simulation parameter values from Table 2, to determine the limiting magnitude for each survey filter (i.e., u-, g-, r-, i-, z-, and Y-band for LSST, and g-, r-, and i-band for ZTF), over the set of possible angular rates (75 angular rates equally spaced between 0.10.10.1 and 1.01.01.0 arcseconds per second). 2.5 Translating MEGASIM Orbits to Survey Pointings

Of the 11.2 million MEGASIM initialized orbits, only those stable for a minimum of 15,000 years (∼similar-to\sim368,287 ETA orbits) were propagated through the survey simulations. This propagation uses a maximum time step of 30 seconds, and is integrated to the exact time of each simulated LSST and ZTF observation time141414The time of each simulated observation comes from OpSim for our simulated LSST surveys, and real observation times are taken for our simulated ZTF survey. If observation times are shorter than the 30 second timestep, then we integrate to the exact timestep of the exposure. To simulate how many MEGASIM ETAs could be detected in the LSST and ZTF surveys, we propagate the simulation forwards or backwards from t0subscript𝑡0t_{0}=JD2459145.61625984 (the time that our surveys are initialized at) to the start date of the respective surveys; for LSST, forward propagation was done to sync with the start date of the LSST OpSim, and backward propagation was done to sync the Solar System with the beginning of the ZTF survey. The shifting of initialization resulted in on-sky errors of the order 10−6superscript10610^{-6}° in right ascension (R.A.) and 6×10−76superscript1076\times 10^{-7}° in declination (Dec.). A maximum error in pointing of 2.25 arcseconds in R.A. and Dec. occurred over the course of the entire simulation. Considering the effective field-of-view diameters of 1.75° for LSST and 3.4° for ZTF, the deviations between the simulated and true positions do not significantly affect results.

Once the MEGASIM simulated Solar System reaches the timestamp of a given survey pointing, a check is done to determine which ETAs are at an R.A. and Dec. within the observations field-of-view. To simplify this check, a circle is used to approximate the field-of-view of LSST and a square for ZTF. The effective circle for LSST is taken to be of radius 1.75°, and for ZTF, the square has side lengths of 3.428° and an area of 47 sq degrees. ETAs found within a survey’s field-of-view are then cataloged. A further check is then done to determine if the ETA in the field-of-view is bright enough to be detected by the telescope. If the ETA apparent magnitude is brighter than the 5σ𝜎\sigma sensitivity for that exposure, it is cataloged as a detection.

2.6 Generating Detection Statistics

To place statistical weights on ETA detections in the LSST and ZTF surveys, detections in each survey simulation were recalculated by assigning all ETAs a new magnitude (H𝐻H), diameter (Dastsubscript𝐷astD_{\text{ast}}), and albedo (pvsubscript𝑝𝑣p_{v}). New ETA properties are obtained via resampling the H𝐻H and pvsubscript𝑝𝑣p_{v} distributions laid out in §2.2 , enabling ETA resampling and detection determination in post without needing to re-simulate all of the ETA trajectories. This resampling was completed 1000 times for each LSST Opsim and ZTF pointing. In each step of the resampling, a unique detection list was created following the procedure described above.

3 Distributions of ETAs

3.1 MEGASIM coordinates

The Cartesian coordinates of the MEGASIM are aligned with aspects of the Solar System as it was on t0subscript𝑡0t_{0}=JD2459145.61625984. The X-axis is aligned with the vernal equinox at simulation time t𝑡t = t0subscript𝑡0t_{0}, with both the X- and Y-axis in the plane of the ecliptic. The Z-axis is aligned with the ecliptic north pole. We define a rotating ecliptic spherical coordinate system centered on Earth. The Sun’s latitude and longitude are always at the origin in this rotating frame, and the idealized L4 point is at (0°, 60°). This simplifies the analysis by allowing us to simply stack parallel simulations with one another, while also satisfying the goal of forecasting ETA observability.

3.2 Stacked Distribution Maps

Figure 1 shows the number density of all ETA positions stacked in 100 kyr intervals, up to 1 Gyr. As ETAs are removed from the MEGASIM due to instability, they no longer contribute to these maps. Unstable orbits are more numerous, but offer significantly shorter trajectories than the few orbits that are stable on long timescales. This figure is presented in the ecliptic rotating coordinate system discussed above. The black circle is the L4 point (0°,60°), and the red cross is the bin with the highest number of ETAs. The peak density is 7° Sun-ward, offset from L4. These results are largely consistent with a similar figure from Wiegert et al. (2000), but with a much higher resolution. The stacked 2D histograms reveal the large on-sky area over which ETAs can travel, but for the most part, ETAs are found within 10° of the ecliptic. There are several bands of higher and lower stability, which are more evident in later figures with different coordinates presented. Worth noting are the ridges and gaps above and below the ecliptic plane at longitudes between ∼similar-to\sim20° and ∼similar-to\sim50°, as well as wings that flare away from the ecliptic at longitudes beyond L4, as these features have not been resolved in previous simulations.

Weighting the stacked ETA trajectories from Figure 1 by their apparent magnitude has only a minor effect in determining where they may be observed. If the ETA on-sky positions are weighted by their flux, the location of the peak density is only shifted by about 0.5°. One-dimensional cuts on ecliptic longitude and latitude of Figure 1 are shown in Figure 3. The notable features are a steep fall off as the ecliptic latitude increases, a local minimum at ∼similar-to\sim40°, and local maximum ∼similar-to\sim35° ecliptic longitude. In Figure 2 we show the difference obtained from the ETA trajectories by weighting observations by brightness (i.e., apparent magnitude), binning data into a 2D histogram, and renormalizing bin values into the range of 0 to 1 and taking the difference (see Figure 1). ETAs interior to L4 are generally fainter than the density map would suggest (hence the blue patch interior to the L4 point). This is due to the majority of ETAs in this portion of the sky existing at fairly far distances from Earth, resulting in lower apparent magnitude. Those found in the region exterior to L4 from the Sun tend to be close to their nearest approach to Earth on their trajectory. The phase angle in the brightest region does result in a reduction of apparent magnitude, but the effect is sub-dominate to the proximity effect with Earth. For observing considerations, the region behind L4 is much easier to observe due to the greater solar elongation (compared to trying to observe the generally fainter ETAs interior on the sky to L4).

Figure 4 shows the projected density of stacked ETA positions in the ecliptic plane, and reveals that ETA trajectories extend into the Solar System as far as the orbit of Venus, spanning the full range of longitudes from Earth to L3. As one would expect, the highest ETA densities are found around L4, but slightly counterclockwise from L4, which agrees with the location indicated in Figure 1. Measuring an angle counter-clockwise from Earth (the blue dot at the top of Figure 4), the highest density of ETAs is located about 5° from L4, towards the Earth. ETA density around the peak remains within 10% of the peak as close as 45° out to about 80° from Earth. From 90° to 105°, there is a region of reduced ETA density before another local maximum in ETA density found near 115-120° from Earth.

3.3 Distribution of Billion Year Stable ETAs

Figure 5 shows single snapshots in time, for only those ETAs that have survived at least 1 Gyr of the MEGASIM integration. The top panels are the initial positions of all billion year (Gyr) stable ETAs, and the bottom panels are 100 Myr after the initial positions. To state the most obvious first, Gyr-stable ETAs are of low inclination and do not extend beyond 10°ecliptic latitude. The spatial distribution, unsurprisingly, does not span far from that of Earth’s orbit. The same density ridges and gaps seen in Figure 4 persist in the Gyr stable orbits. The tail of the ETA distribution is within the drawn circular Earth orbit at 100 Myr, shown in the bottom right panel of Figure 5. Other snapshots in time reveal the distribution of ETAs oscillates in and outside of the drawn circular orbit of Earth, at a frequency higher than the simulation output of 1000 years. This indicates that there is a bulk forcing of the ETA population throughout their libration trajectories i.e., all ETAs are moving together as a relatively cohesive unit, and that distribution wobbles around together with a wave like pattern, as they move with Earth in its orbit).

4 Results: The Detection Prediction of ETAs in ZTF and LSST Surveys

4.1 Detection Statistics and Cumulative Counts

Figure 6 shows the percentage of ETAs by filter type, split between the ZTF survey (blue), LSST twilight survey (yellow), and LSST baseline survey (green). The r-band filter for LSST and g-band filter for ZTF are the most successful at detecting ETAs, and ZTF’s g-band detects more ETAs than LSST’s g-band. Of the 706,459 ZTF pointings used, 38.57% (272,541) were in g-band, 56.99% (402,632) in r-band, and 4.43% (31,286) in i-band. The ratio of the number of g-band and r-band observations by LSST are of unity but g-band provides an order of magnitude fewer detections than ZTF.

The rate ZTF detects ETAs is roughly the same as the baseline LSST OpSim result. The simulated twilight LSST survey captures approximately four times the number of ETA detections as the LSST baseline or ZTF.

The number of distinct detections per asteroid, per survey, is shown in Figure 10. There is a stark difference between the two LSST surveys. The baseline LSST survey recovers many asteroids numerous times (as many as 2000 times, which is far more than typical NEAs are recovered), but not nearly as many as the LSST OpSim twilight survey results. 4.4 The Yarkovsky Effect and ETA Detections

In the MEGASIM, only gravitational forces were used to propagate the ETA orbits. Here we estimate the number of gravitationally stable ETAs that may be driven unstable by non-gravitational effects. The dominant additional force to consider is the Yarkovsky Effect (e.g., Chesley et al., 2003), which causes asteroids to rotate and increase their semi-major axis due to Solar radiation pressure. Simulations of ETA orbits with the Yarkovsky Effect included were done by Zhou et al. (2019). The Yarkovsky Effect’s strength depends on the shape, albedo distribution, surface conductivity, spin, and density of a given asteroid. The additional force from the Yarkovsky Effect was found to remove ETAs within 1 Gyr, for ETAs with diameters smaller than 130 meter or 90 meter, for prograde and retrograde rotation, respectively (Zhou et al., 2019).

To estimate the changes to our survey completeness curves that the Yarkovsky Effect would impose, a cut-off on the simulated ETA population was made using both the lifetime and diameter of a given ETA. To estimate the removal rate of gravitationally stable ETAs via the Yarkovsky Effect in the MEGASIM, we use an ETA diameter cutoff (Dunstablesubscript𝐷unstableD_{\text{unstable}}) provided by Equation 7.

where l​i​f​e​t​i​m​e100​m𝑙𝑖𝑓𝑒𝑡𝑖𝑚subscript𝑒100𝑚lifetime_{100m} is the time a 100 meter diameter ETA remained bound to L4 in the MEGASIM for one Gyr. Equation 7 is a simple approximation for the ETA diameter cutoff, given the results of Zhou et al. We assume this linear approximation to estimate the number of smaller ETAs affected on shorter time scales. In a few percent of the resampled runs, some ETAs with lifetimes greater than 750 Myr should have been removed due to the Yarkovsky Effect. However, the number of ETAs detected in the resamples, within a confidence interval of 68%, were essentially unaffected by the Yarkovsky effect, as it is not strong enough to drastically change our estimated survey detections at these timescales. This is due to the fact that our detected ETAs are either too large (i.e., the diameter of ETAs just large enough to be detected by LSST are also just above the diameter for which the Yarkovsky effect will produce orbital instability), or have not been around long enough for the Yarkovsky Effect to manifest instability (i.e. Gyr timescales). ZTF is not included because there are no timescales where ZTF detections remain stable after this procedure. We reiterate that this is due to small number statistics in our bootstrap populations throughout the detection efficiency analysis above.

5 Conclusion

The work presented here can be categorized into three areas. 1) The spatial distributions of MEGASIM ETA trajectories, 2) the detectability of MEGASIM ETAs by LSST and ZTF, and 3) an upper limit of an actual ETA population assuming a null detection.

5.1 ETA Spatial Distributions

When viewing L4 ETAs from Earth, the highest on-sky concentration is found 6.5° closer to the Sun than the L4 Lagrange point. 30% of ETA trajectories are found within 45-60° Solar elongation and another 30% are found beyond 60°. 70% of all ETAs exist within 5° of the ecliptic plane across all longitudes. Detection of ETAs from the ground is challenging inside L4, and detections cannot be made at all times of the year due to the Earth’s axial tilt (Whiteley & Tholen, 1998). Observations require longer exposure times due to higher sky brightness at twilight, and higher airmass than typical observations at opposition.

The distribution of ETAs as viewed looking down the pole of the ecliptic plane shows a toroidal volume that tapers near the Earth and L3 Lagrange point. ETAs can librate in as far as the orbit of Venus, and out as far as 1.3 AU, especially if they are only stable for short periods of time. ETAs that are stable longer mostly stay within 0.98 - 1.02 AU of the Sun, and in the ecliptic plane, the highest density of ETAs oscillates over a considerable distance. High densities occur along a ridge spanning 45° leading Earth’s orbit to 90°, with the peak within a few degrees of the L4 point (if averaged over time). ETAs have long libration periods (decades to millennia), so the time it takes to oscillate from behind the Sun to closer toward the Earth could fall completely outside of the survey observation window, meaning that modern surveys for observing the ETA population may not be feasible..

As the lifetime of ETAs increases, the region that surviving ETAs traverse tightens onto the Earth’s orbit. ETAs that are stable over long periods of time do not approach Earth or L3 as closely, and remain in a tighter bunch around L4. This is one reason for fewer detections of longer-term stable ETAs in Figure 16, even for ZTF and the LSST twilight survey, which are notionally designed for such low Solar elongation observations.

The on-sky location where ETAs are brightest does not correlate to the most likely position on the sky. The brightest on-sky areas are found at Solar elongations outside of L4, at angles of 60° to 75°. The brightest location is on the ecliptic, but elevated brightness regions spread beyond L4 to latitudes of ±20plus-or-minus20\pm 20. This is apparent in Figure 2 as a flaring yellow regime to the right of L4, and is due to the improved observing geometry.

5.2 Ability of LSST and ZTF to Detect ETAs

Due to the latitude that the telescopes for ZTF and LSST are located, the time of year in which each can detect L4 ETAs differs (see Figure 9). ZTF finds most ETAs from June to December, and LSST from January to June. In our simulations, ZTF was unsuccessful at detecting the vast majority of the ETA population, due to limited photometric sensitivity (H<19𝐻19H<19 for most detections). Of the ZTF detections, only 4 unique ETAs were observed. Though, due to the field-of-view of the ZTF telescope, repeat detections are on the order of thousands, which indicates that any detected ETA by ZTF would be very well characterized and tracked.

Both the LSST baseline and twilight survey recovered the same number of unique ETAs, 188. However, the twilight survey was able to make several thousand repeat detections, with 4 of the asteroids observed over 10,000 times. These results show that a twilight survey will not increase the likelihood of individual ETA detections drastically compared to the baseline survey. Though, a twilight survey would provide us with several orders of magnitude more observations of select ETAs. Observing asteroids on the order of thousands to tens of thousands of times is unprecedented and would be useful for constraining characterization of individual asteroids.

5.3 The Possible ETA Population

ZTF did not have a statistically significant number of detections to provide meaningful constraints on the upper limit of the ETA population, for any size range. That is, at magnitude H<𝐻absentH< 18, the probability that an ETA exists become so low that ZTF’s observing cadence will not detect the ETA frequently enough to count as an observed ETA. However, both the LSST baseline and twilight surveys provided enough detections to estimate an upper limit for ETAs, of Myr up to Gyr stability times. Due to the Yarkovsky Effect, C-type and S-type ETAs of absolute magnitudes greater than H=22𝐻22H=22 and H=24𝐻24H=24, respectively, will be driven unstable within 1 Gyr. The rarity of ETAs greater than 100 meters in diameter, paired with the fact that smaller asteroids will be driven unstable, our results indicate that a null detection by LSST will mean that there are no remaining Gyr-stable or primordial ETA populations. On the other hand, the existing ETA discoveries are indicative of a small and transient population. A null detection in LSST will restrict that population to tens of objects larger than 100 meters.
