                                  I.   INTRODUCTION


   Thanks to recent advances in observational astrophysics and strong interaction theory,
interest in studying compact stars as a tool for understanding high density matter has
been rejuvenated [3–8]. Recent theoretical advances have challenged the Bodmer-Witten-
Terazawa hypothesis [9–11] by showing that up-down quark matter (udQM) might be the
ground state of baryonic matter, and thus more stable than even strange quark matter
(SQM) [8]. In addition to this, several objects in tension with simple neutron equations of
state coupled to general relativity (like PSR J0030+0451 [3], PSR J0740+6620 [4], HESS
J1731-347 [5], and GW190814 [6]) have in particular led to a number of articles on quark
stars in recent years [1, 2, 12–23].
    In 2021 Zhang, Gammon, and Mann looked at the structure and stability of quark stars
with a unified interacting equation of state and a net charge [2], presenting a host of stable
charged compact objects with strong interaction effects accounted for in the equation of
state [12]. However, this paper made use of a stability equation for charged compact objects
[1] that contained an error. This error, evidently a typo, does not affect the mass/radius
relation for charged quark stars at all. However the stability properties of these stars can
change significantly from what was reported in [2].
   In this comment article we investigate the stability of charged, interacting quark stars,
making use of the corrected stability equations. In section II we briefly review the origin of
the error and discuss how the equations governing stability change as a result. In section
III we reproduce the results from [2] with the corrected stability data for a positive strong
quark interaction constant λ. The following section correspondingly covers the negative
λ results. We also discuss the implications of the changes in the results compared to
the original study [2]. Section V summarizes qualitative trends from the preceding two
sections, and finally section VI is used for a summary and concluding remarks. For a more
thorough look at the theory governing the structure and stability of charged interacting
quark stars we direct the reader to [2].

   From here, as before, radial stability of these stars can be determined by the sign of
the fundamental mode eigenfrequency ω02 - radial stability is achieved when ω02 ≥ 0. For
uncharged compact stars, the point at which the fundamental radial mode eigenfrequency
switches signs is always coincident with the point where ∂M/∂ρc changes sign (here ρc is
the central mass density of a star, a boundary condition). The result of this is the zero
fundamental eigenfrequency point coinciding with the curve’s maximum mass point.
   For charged compact stars this coincidence is no longer guaranteed. The specifics of
this change (whether the stability transition point moves to higher/lower central densities,
and by how much) depend on the model chosen for the distribution of charge, and the
strength of the strong interaction parameter λ. We use the following section to share these
specifics.

EOSs coupled to general relativity, and thus they are interesting in the context of exotic
compact objects. More specifically, in figs. 1 to 6 the coloured boxes are 1σ observational
estimates of mass/radii for PSR J0030+0451 (violet) [3], PSR J0740+6620 (yellow) [4],

                          III.   RESULTS FOR POSITIVE λ


   Here we present the results describing the stellar structure and the radial stability
for charged, interacting quark stars with a positive strong interaction constant λ. In all
cases the mass/radius results are unchanged (matching those in [2]); because of this we
primarily comment on the location of the ‘stability point’ (the point at which ω02 changes
signs) relative to the maximum mass point along the mass-radius curve.


                                  A.   Charge Model A


   Charge model A assumes a proportionality

                                          ρe = αρ

between mass density and charge density [1, 2, 22, 26] (or, in unitless form, ρ̄e = αρ̄ - see
appendix A). These results are shown along with the associated M /R and M/ρc curves in
figure 1.
    As before, the stability point coincides with the maximum mass point for the uncharged
solution, and as before when charge is introduced the stability point separates from the
maximum mass point and moves to a star with smaller central density. We find, however,
that the relative size of the gap is much smaller than previously reported [2]. This means
that more of these stars are stable than we previously thought. The effect of increasing λ
is a suppression of the separation induced by the charge, both in [2] and in figure 1.
   Furthermore, contrary to the previous study [2], we also see a turnaround behaviour in
the stability point/maximum mass separation. By the time we get to very large charge
(α = 0.999) the stability point has returned to coincidence with the maximum mass point,
regardless of λ - this is qualitatively different behaviour than what was predicted in [2],
where the separation continued to grow with charge all the way to α = 0.999. Once
again we note that there are more stable stars in the parameter space than was previously
thought.

   Commensurate with the previous study [2], charge model B offsets the stability point
in the opposite direction relative to charge model A (namely to larger central densities)
as a nonzero charge parameter is introduced. Here this separation continues growing as
β increases - we do not observe the turnaround behaviour that was present for model A,
meaning that the largest separation between maximum mass and the stability point is seen
at the largest β considered.
   Furthermore, the previous study [2] reported that the size of the central density sep-
                                                                                                           7

aration gets rapidly larger, then slowly becomes smaller as λ̄ increases. We also observe
similar behaviour here - though difficult to tell by eye, in figure 2 the central density sep-
aration also follows this trend (namely growing from λ̄ = 0 → λ̄ = 0.5, and then shrinking
for larger λ). In general we also see a larger average separation size, which means the
sequence contains more stable stars than reported in [2]. We also note the truncations of
the curves in the upper right panel, which appear because of the exotic pressure profiles
associated with charge model B (ie. the pressure first increases above its central value pc
before decreasing to 0 at finite radius). The net effect is that the M /R curves do not start
from the origin. This behaviour was also noted in the previous study [2] where a more
thorough discussion is included.
   Finally, the curves for largest β were originally found to have no stability point for large
enough λ̄ [2] – it was reported that the fundamental mode eigenfrequency was negative
for all solutions along such curves. As we see in figure 2 this is no longer the case – all
branches have at least some stable solutions, including those branches that don’t start from
the origin.


                                         C.    Charge Model C


    In this case we fix the total charge, whose actual values (Q̄ = (0, 1.538, 3.076) × 10−2 ,
corresponding to Q = (0, 1, 2)×1020 C for a typical bag constant value Beff = 60 MeV/fm3 )
are chosen to be commensurate with past work [1, 2]. The corresponding results are
obtained by enumerating the charge configurations and central densities that yield the
chosen fixed charge value for a given λ̄. This can be implemented with interior charge
distributions of type model A or model B; we choose to use the former model (in line with
[2]) for the results in figure 31 , which is consistent with the stability points shifting to
lower central densities rather than higher. As in model B, some of the curves truncate at
smaller mass/central pressure. This is due to charge model C fixing the star’s total charge
rather than the charge model parameter α/β. Because of this, an arbitrarily small star
can’t necessarily support a large predetermined charge. The curves are thus ‘cut off’ when
no solutions have the required total charge.
   Here the separations are much smaller than the equivalent plot in [2] (which aligns
with the results in III A), which indicates that there are more stable stars than previously
reported. In the present work the fixed charges are too small to appreciably offset the
stability point from the maximum mass point - the differences we do see are on the order
of < 0.1% for central densities, < 0.01% for the radii, and < 10−7 for the unitless masses.

                          IV.   RESULTS FOR NEGATIVE λ


   In the previous treatment of model A with λ < 0 [2], the added charge was found to
always induce a separation between the two points of interest, and as the magnitude of
(negative) λ grew, an increase was seen in the separation between points in central density
space. The same behaviour is seen here, but with much smaller density separations on
average than those presented in [2] (see figure 4). As expected in model A, the maximum
mass points are at larger central densities than the stability points (in both [2] and the
present work). This density separation concurrently manifests as a decrease in separation
in M /R space in [2], which we find is also the case here, just on smaller separation scales.
This isn’t inconsistent with the former result as a large change in central density doesn’t
necessarily correspond to a large change in mass. However, unlike [2], we once again see
that the effect of large charge in model A is to force the separation back to 0, even when
λ is negative and has a large magnitude. In the previous study [2] the large charge limit
was found to have very low differences in central density between maximum mass and
stability points, but had a large mass separation between those same points. Employing
the corrected stability equations, we do not observe this. These new predictions allow for
more stable stars that are strongly charged.
   For model B with λ < 0, introducing charge was found to offset the stability point to
higher central densities [2] (as it does here), but only appreciably so when the magnitude
of λ is small. This means that making λ more negative here suppresses the separation
induced by charge model B. We see analogous behaviour in figure 5, but with larger average
separations than those in [2]. This means that relative to [2] we predict more stable stars
in the sequence.
   When λ < 0, the results for charge model C in the previous investigation [2] indicated
that separations between maximum mass and stability points are made larger as the mag-
nitude of λ increases, in concert with the charge, which also induces separation. Together
this is manifest as charge effects being most effective when λ has a large magnitude and is
negative. In our updated analysis we find that the behaviour is similar (see figure 6), but
with the separation sizes generally being much smaller (again leading to more of the stars
being stable than we previously thought).
    We have again included the four recent observational constraints (not present in [2]) in
all of our M /R diagrams for the λ < 0 case as well. However in this case we find that
the observed mass-radius constraints for all four objects are not always consistent with
the curves we obtain for the different charge models, due to negative λ pushing results to
smaller masses and radii).

                                    V.   DISCUSSION


    We have analyzed the stability properties of charged quark stars using equation (10)
with Q given by (13), correcting the equation employed in a previous study [1, 2]. The
individual cases (consisting of charge model, charge strength, and size/sign of λ) in general
differ in their effects on the separation between the stability point and maximum mass
point, but some general trends can be observed.
   First, the relative direction that the stability point moves with respect to the maximum
mass point is unchanged from what was previously reported [2]. In model A (and C,
which implicitly uses model A) the stability points move toward smaller central densities,
whereas in model B they move to larger central densities. The size of the induced separation
between the two points in general differs from what was obtained previously in [2].
   In general the separation size in model A increases as the charge parameter α increases,
but eventually turns around and closes for maximal α. Making λ more positive in these
cases suppresses this separation, and accordingly making λ more negative can contribute
to the separation. Barring the turnaround at large charge which is unique to the present
investigation, these general trends are in accordance with the earlier work [2] (however the
separation sizes are smaller than previously reported in [2]).
   For model B, separation size also increases as β increases but we do not observe any
kind of turnaround behaviour in the range of β considered. The effects of λ are slightly
more complicated than the model A case – making λ more positive first contributes to
an increased separation from λ̄ = 0 → λ̄ = 0.5, but then suppresses separation when
increased further. On the other hand, making λ more negative strictly suppresses the
separation induced by β. It is worth noting that [2] also saw this turnaround behaviour
for λ > 0 under charge model B [2], and negative λ also had a suppressing effect there.
The most notable difference is the fact that in this work all model B branches have stable
stars, including when λ → ∞ (which was not the case in [2]).
   In general the separation sizes for the fixed charges considered in model C are very small
in the present paper and are hardly perceptible on an M /R plot, but since these solutions
are implicitly using model A, we know that the qualitative effects of λ and (small) charge
parameter α in both our study and [2] match those already discussed for model A. The
unique feature here (in contrast with [2]) is that once again all branches contain stable star
solutions, even those starting relatively near the maximum mass point.


                                   VI.   CONCLUSION


   We have numerically recalculated the stability properties of charged interacting quark
stars, with the stability equations from [1] (propagating to [2]) corrected. Many of the
general trends of the parameter space remain unchanged, but the actual magnitudes of the
separations between the stability point and maximum mass can change significantly.
   The most interesting qualitative changes to the results include “turnaround” behaviour
of the separation which reapproaches 0 for maximal model A charge parameter α, and
the fact that model B branches which were entirely unstable in [2] all contain stable star
                                                                                              15

solutions in the corrected results. In all cases these changes allow for more stable stars
than was reported in [2].
   It is clear from figs. 1 to 6 that, for particular choices of the strong interaction parameter
(such as λ > 0 and 0 ≤ λ̄ ≤ 1), a subset of these solutions are stable and also pass through
all four of the included observational constraints. A larger number of stable solutions
satisfy only some of the constraints, and a larger amount still satisfy none. There is no
guarantee that these objects actually are (charged) quark stars, however if they are, the
data is consistent with an interacting equation of state for particular values of the strong
interaction parameter λ.
